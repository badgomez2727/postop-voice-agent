of occurrence can vary considerably according to surgical indication, time elapsed between

10 to 15% in the overall population, it varies pronouncedly
from one country to another [1]. Around 30% of patients pre-
senting with biliary lithiasis encounter complications, with
annual incidence ranging from 1 to 3% [2]. In the United
States, prevalence of symptomatic vesicular lithiasis has
doubled over the course of the last three decades [3]. In

Rehospitalization rates and risk factors
Rates of rehospitalization following cholecystectomy range
from 0 to 11%; 7% worldwide according to the different series
and definitions (generally 30 or 90 days) [8]. Quite obvi-
ously, socio-economic factors characterizing the countries
and populations included, not to mention disparate medical
practices, affect these rates. In France, rehospitalization
rates are not published on a nationwide scale, with the
exception of a French multicenter prospective series con-
cerning the randomized ABCAL trial on acute cholecystitis,
which reported 7.5% of rehospitalizations after 414 chole-
cystectomies for acute cholecystitis [9].
The main causes for rehospitalization have been doc-
umented in a large-scale register study by Rosero et al.
on ambulatory procedures [10]. They are highlighted by
residual gallstones in the common bile duct (CBD) (20%),
most of which are managed by endoscopy (90%). The other
causes encompass pain (16%), surgical site infections (14%),
nausea/vomiting (12%) and hemorrhagic complications (8%).
Biliary wounds account for 0.008% of reasons for readmis-
sion.
Risk factors for rehospitalizations were specifically
analyzed in the same register studies [8,10,11]. Some
comorbidities including chronic organ failure (chronic renal
failure, chronic pulmonary pathologies, chronic hepatopa-
thy) and progressive cancer, as well as demographic
characteristics such as male gender, advanced age, ethnic
identity (non-Hispanic white) and type of insurance (non-
private) have been identified in American cohorts as risk
factors for rehospitalization; on the contrary, obesity is
not a documented risk factor. Among surgical risk factors,
acute cholecystitis is clearly associated with excess mor-
bidity [11] and an increased risk of rehospitalization (7.5%
according to the ABCAL trial [9]); cholecystectomy per-
formed during the weekend is likewise associated with an
increased risk of rehospitalization, indirectly attesting to
the degree of emergency and severity [10,11]. While single-
port surgery (vs. four trocars) and ambulatory procedures
were not associated with lower readmission rates [8], pre-
cise data concerning the impact of surgical approach are few
and far between.
Non-specific causes of rehospitalization
Postoperative discomfort
The laparoscopic approach helps to reduce postoperative
discomfort, which can associate pain, nausea and vomiting.
However, discomfort can be sufficiently substantial to affect
the recovery process, for example by delaying departure
from the recovery room, preventing ambulatory manage-
ment or delaying the patient’s discharge, and possibly
occasioning rehospitalization [12]. Discomfort prevention
is the sole guarantee of a decreased risk of rehospital-
ization. Moreover, reduction of postoperative pain and,
consequently, of analgesic consumption, is important, even
in minor abdominal surgery, one reason being that wor-
risome rates of opioid dependency has been observed
following cholecystectomy [13]. In clinical practice, intra-
venous injection of dexamethasone limits the risk of nausea
and vomiting [14]. Perioperatively, systematic infiltration
of trocar-related scars by local anesthesia (carried out
if possible before incision of the skin) is recommended,
especially insofar as it helps to reduce initial analgesic
consumption [15]. By means of a local anti-inflammatory
effect, infiltration helps to limit the phenomenon of self-
induced pain, thereby producing a beneficial effect during
a period outlasting persistence of the product at the
site of administration. Intraperitoneal instillation of a
local anesthetic is frequently proposed, even though its
interest has yet to be clearly demonstrated [15]. Mini-
laparoscopic cholecystectomy is the most widely evaluated
mini-invasive approach, and among selected patients, it
appears to decrease the incidence of postoperative pain
[16,17]. This approach can be combined with the utilization
of reheated and humidified constant pressure CO 2 insuf-
flators, which function at low pressure (9—10 mmHg) and
thereby not only attenuate the pain associated with dis-
tension of the diaphragm and the abdominal wall, but also
avoid the hypothermia and peritoneal irritation contributing
to postoperative pain [18]. These multimodal mini-invasive
approaches are of particular interest for globally fragile
and sickle cell anemia patients [19]. While postopera-
tive pain management necessitates analgesics with levels
adapted to pain intensity, it is recommended to con-
comitantly associate a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug
helping to limit the consumption of analgesics and to lower
the incidence of nausea and vomiting, without increasing
the risk of (hemorrhagic. . .) postoperative complications
[14]. Moreover, rigorous postoperative assessment of the
patient’s overall condition, potentially using a scale such
as APAIS, can help to identify patients at risk of develop-
ing chronic postoperative pain and who are likely to draw
benefit from detailed preoperative information and partic-
ularly rigorous peri-operative and postoperative follow-up
[14].
More generally, enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS),
including immediate ambulation, same-day refeeding,
absence of urinary or abdominal drainage. . .) is practically
systematically 	applied 	after 	laparoscopic 	cholecystec-
tomy and decreases the risk of rehospitalization due
to non-specific causes: fever associated with atelectasis
or pulmonary infection, urinary infection, lymphangitis,
venous thrombosis. . . Mechanical or medicine-based throm-
boprophylaxis (intermittent pneumatic compression) is not
recommended in ambulatory surgery or ERAS in a patient
at low thromboembolic risk (based on the Caprini score),
especially insofar as early deambulation and hydration help
to diminish thrombotic risk [20].

Just like all other postoperative infections, surgical site
infection, at times intraperitoneal or characterized by an
abscess at the site of extraction of the surgical speci-

be prevented by strict application of preventive measures
during the preoperative, perioperative and postoperative
periods: hand hygiene, skin preparation, (non-systematic
[21]) antibioprophylaxis and maintained normothermia [21].
More specifically, contamination of the intestinal wall dur-
ing extraction of the surgical specimen can more often than
not be avoided by, before extraction, placing the retrieved
specimen in a bag, or else, by protecting the abdominal wall
with a skirt [22]. In acute cholecystitis, bacteriological sam-
pling of gallbladder bile is mandatory, especially in case of
Grade II severity according to the Tokyo Guidelines and/or a
complicated form: necroses, emphysema, perforation [23].
In the postoperative phase, continuation of antibiotic ther-
apy for more than 24 hours is not recommended, except in
severe cases (Grade III according to the Tokyo Guidelines
and/or in complicated forms), and treatment will be main-
tained for at least four to seven days in the event of a deep
infectious focus following surgery (hepatic abscess) [23]. In
case of gallbladder perforation, surgery necessarily involves
washing of the surgical site and recovery (if possible) if all
the extracted gallstones. Even in the event of perforation,
drainage is not systematic [24].
Intestinal wall access is treated by surgical debridement.
Management of deep abscess is more complex and may asso-
ciate radiologic or endoscopic drainage and even, in certain
cases, surgical redo (by laparoscopic approach, if possible)
according to the positioning of the collection and the avail-
able technical platforms [25,26]. Antibiotherapy is generally
initiated prior to drainage, adapted according to the results
of the bacteriological sampling, and maintained for five to
fifteen days [26]. Deep abscesses are generally lacking in
precise cause; that said, they may in some cases be entailed
by a biliary fistula (see below) or, more rarely, by ‘‘lost’’
intra-abdominal or parietal gallstones, which shall need to
be extracted, along with the abscess, in order to avoid pos-
sible recurrence. Gallstone ablation may be simple, when
the gallstone has migrated in the wall, or more complex,
necessitating percutaneous or surgical radiological ablation
or more complicated surgery [27].
Hemorrhages
Hemorrhagic complications are relatively rare (2—3%), and
may be divided into occasionally severe perioperative
hemorrhage (less than 2%) [28] and postoperative hem-
orrhage, accounting for 1 to 2% of post-cholecystectomy
complications, and responsible for 8% of rehospitalizations
[8,28], encompassing hematomas of the vascular bed and/or
wall. The risk of hemorrhagic complications is compounded
by thromboprophylaxis [28,29], which nonetheless does not
reduce thromboembolic complications; as a result, it is not
systematically recommended. Moreover, the laparoscopic
approach is associated with heightened hemorrhagic risk
[30], which reflects the degree of surgical difficulty and
severity of the vesicular pathology.
Management of postoperative hematomas depends on
degree of severity, extent of impact, and the patient’s
overall condition; it ranges from simple monitoring in a
patient in good general condition presenting with a small
hematoma without hemodynamic or biological repercus-
sions, to surgical redo in the event of voluminous hematoma
responsible for deglobulization. A postoperative hematoma
is at risk of surgical site infection and must in all cases
be closely monitored. Particular vigilance is required for
patients undergoing hemostasis-modifying treatment (anti-
coagulants, antiaggregants), and it begins with painstaking
surgical hemostasis of the vascular bed and the trocar
insertion points, especially those on the trajectory of the
epigastric artery.
Residual gallstone
In a large-scale American registry study, recurrent symptoms
associated with a residual gallstone represent the second
leading cause of rehospitalization subsequent to cholecys-
tectomy [31]. Persistent gallstones expose the patient to
the risk of angiocholitis and/or pancreatitis associated with
a long-term increase in morbidity and mortality [32]. While
risk of residual gallstone is directly related to the initial
lithiasic pathology (presence during the cholecystectomy
of one or more gallstones in the CBD, or previous lithia-
sic migration, angiocholitis or pancreatitis), it may also be
impacted by type of preoperative exploration (liver function
test, endoscopy or biliary MRI), perioperative management
(with or without cholangiography) or, quite obviously, when
gallstones are initially present in the CBD, by the means of
their management (preoperative or perioperative ERCP or
surgical ablation the gallstone).
Any suspected gallstone in the CBD (abnormalities on the
liver function test not explained by the clinical picture, or
abnormal dilation of the bile ducts observed on ultrasound)
necessitates preoperative or perioperative exploration of
the CBD, the objective being to avoid leaving behind a resid-
ual gallstone. Prior to surgery, biliary MRI or endoscopic
ultrasound may be considered. Biliary MRI is advantageous
insofar as it does not necessitate general anesthesia and
presents high sensitivity and specificity [33]. Endoscopic
ultrasound not only presents equivalent sensitivity and
specificity [33], but also enables, if necessary, elimination
of a non-lithiasic cause of obstruction (ampullae, pancreatic
tumor), and possibly even to perform, during general anes-
thesia, ERPC or extraction of a gallstone. The non-invasive
nature of biliary MRI has impelled some teams to have it
routinely included in preoperative protocols. While this atti-
tude may potentially reduce the risk of rehospitalization due
to residual gallstone, its cost—benefit ratio remains to be
determined [34].
The possible interest of systematic perioperative cholan-
giography in reducing the risk of residual gallstone has been
widely debated in the literature. Routine use of this rela-
tively non-invasive test, which is not always simple to apply,
has been recommended (or suggested) by several learned
societies; that said, the literature is highly heterogeneous
[35]. In the largest meta-analysis (440,659 patients), the
authors observed no reduction of this risk in the cholangiog-
raphy group [36]. The interest of systematic perioperative
cholangiography in diminishing the risk of MBD injury will be
considered below.
How cholecystectomy is performed has a potential
impact on risk of residual gallstone, which is according to
several reports greater following laparoscopic surgery than
after open surgery [32,37,38]. Subtotal cholecystectomy,
which may be of interest in the event of difficult dissec-
tion during management of chronic cholecystitis, may entail
the risk of leaving in place a residual gallstone in gallbladder
remnant. Systematic ablation of gallstones during the proce-
dure is consequently of prime importance. Moreover, it has
been suggested that leaving a very long (more than 15 mm)
cystic duct in place during cholecystectomy would increase
the risk of residual gallstone [39]. In practice, it is often dif-

As for the postoperative phase, no systematic examina-

Management of residual gallstones will take into account
their position (gallbladder remnants, cystic duct, MBD. . .),
the patient’s overall condition and medical history (age,
previous Roux-en-Y gastric bypass surgery. . .) as well as
the cause of rehospitalization (abscess on residual vesicular
remnants, simple migration, angiocholitis. . .).
A persistent residual gallstone in vesicular remnants
can be responsible for rehospitalization due to abdomi-
nal pain, associated or not with sepsis or jaundice [40].
Imagery (ultrasound generally postoperatively associated
with abdominal scan) highlights a ‘‘cholecystitis’’ aspect of
the remnants. These examinations are often subsequently
completed by biliary MRI and/or echographic endoscopy.
More often than not, initial management involves antibiotic
therapy. If clinical evolution is favorable, one may con-
sider (taking into account the patient’s overall condition,
the available technical platform, and the center’s experi-
ence) different options in view of avoiding recurrence [40]:
redo surgery some time after the acute episode to remove
the residual gallstone, cholecystectomy by laparoscopy or
laparotomy (procedure that is not always simple, and must
be based on precise assessment of the modalities of the ini-
tial operation: experience of the surgeon, descriptions of
the difficulties encountered in the operation report, anato-
mopathological examination of the surgical specimen. . .),
ablation of the residual gallstone by endoscopy (ERCP and
Dormia basket extraction or lithotrity), which presents
the advantage of being less invasive and is of particular
interest for fragile patients, but is not always practica-
ble.
Management of rehospitalization due to symptomatic
residual gallstone in the MBD (simple biliary pain associated
with liver function perturbations, angiocholitis or pancre-
atitis) does not essentially differ from management of any
other MBD gallstone. That said, the following three points
must be kept in mind. First, the earlier the rehospital-
ization, the higher the risk of biliary fistula, due largely
to increased tension in the MBD and, consequently, of
the cystic duct remnants; this should impel the surgical
teams to remove the MBD obstacle as rapidly as possi-
ble, generally by ERCP. Secondly, any persistent gallstone,
particularly in the cystic duct, presents a risk of recur-
rence and must, if at all possible, be extracted. Third,
especially in young patients, symptom recurrence or doubt
concerning the imaged presence of intrahepatic gallstones,
suggests possible genetic predisposition to biliary gallstone
formation (Low-Phospholipid Associated Cholelithiasis) and
necessitates orientation of the patient toward an expert
center to confirm the presence of intrahepatic gallstones
with a ‘‘comet tail’’ aspect, search for a mutation of the
ABCB4gene (inconsistency. . .), and apply ursodeoxycholic
acid treatment.
Bile duct injury
Overall 	incidence 	of 	post-cholecystectomy 	biliary
complications is 1.5% including biliary leaks [41], and
bile duct injury (BDI) occurs at a frequency ranging from
0.15% to 0.36% (major injuries), which is higher than the
rate of 0.1% to 0.2% reported during the epoch of open
cholecystectomy. Cholecystitis remains the main risk factor
for BDI during cholecystectomy. BDI is responsible for excess
morbidity, long-term excess mortality and consequential
costs for healthcare systems, and is also one of the most
common causes of litigation against digestive surgeons.
Rehospitalization due to BDI injury is rare, involving less
than 0.01% of the cases of rehospitalization after chole-
cystectomy. These rare cases correspond to injury that has
not been identified preoperatively, and it may be related
to varied clinical pictures: biloma or choleperitonitis in
an open wound (bile duct or bile canal section), jaun-
dice ± angiocholitis in occlusion wound (clip, staples on the
main bile duct). Management must necessarily be organized
in an expert center and is discussed on a case-by-case basis.
A biliary fistula usually necessitates emergency drainage by
surgery in cases of choleperitonitis, or percutaneously in
cases of biloma. Jaundice does not necessarily entail emer-
gency biliary drainage, except with associated angiocholitis.
Of note, biliary dilatation may represent an anatomical fac-
tor favorable to subsequent surgical repair. After (possible)
drainage and prior to any repair procedure, the topography
of the wound must be painstakingly assessed by means of
imagery (bili-IRM), and associated vascular lesions respon-
sible for slow extension by an ischemic mechanism of the
initial lesions must likewise be evaluated (by angio-TDM).
Repair of the wound itself can be endoscopic, surgical,
immediately or delayed, and choice of the suitable option
depends on several topographic, anatomical and vascular
factors, as well as the patient’s general state of health. A
simple lateral leak may be covered by an endoscopic pros-
thesis, but resection of the convergence with an associated
vascular lesion can entail complex repair by hepaticojeju-
nal anastomosis along multiple ducts and, in some cases,
will necessitate associated hepatectomies; lastly but very
rarely, liver transplant may be required.
Several surgical societies have issued BDI prevention rec-
ommendations [42,43]; here are the main messages.
Diagnostic criteria and preoperative time
lapses in cases of acute cholecystitis favoring
BDI
Due to possibly associated pedicular inflammation, acute
cholecystitis is a major BDI risk factor. The Tokyo recom-
mendations [44,45] classify cholecystitis in the terms of
three levels of severity according to clinico-biological pre-
sentation. On the basis of this classification and in view
of reducing the risk of BDI, it is recommended for Grade
I (mild) patients to perform laparoscopic cholecystectomy
within seven days (ideally in 72 hours) after symptom onset.
As for Grade II (intermediate) patients, emergency laparo-
scopic cholecystectomy is called for, provided that the
patient’s general condition is good, and that laparoscopy
is technically feasible. When the patient’s general state of

Does there exist preoperative imagery
reducing BDI risk?
For the diagnosis of acute cholecystitis without suspected
gallstone migration in the main biliary duct (MBD), ultra-
sound remains the gold standard. In case of suspected
associated MBD gallstone, bili-MRI or endoscopic ultra-
sound ± ERCP may be considered (according to the context)
[43]. Bili-MRI presents several advantages: it is rapid and
non-invasive, it shows good sensitivity and specificity in the
diagnosis of biliary obstruction (> 95%), and it permits the
study of biliary anatomy via reconstruction. While preoper-
ative MRI highlighting of anatomical biliary variations can
potentially prevent BDI risk, this has yet to be scientifically
demonstrated.
Good operative practices: Meeting the Critical
View of Safety (CVS) challenge:
It has now been demonstrated that when obtained perioper-
atively, CVS status is associated with a decreased risk of BDI
[42]. As described by Strasberg et al., CVS must encompass
the three following elements: (1) the hepatocytic triangle
is not obstructed by adipose or fibrous tissue; (2) the lower
third of the gall bladder is separated from the liver, thereby
exposing the cystic plate; (3) two and only two structures
entering the gall bladder must be visualized [46]. In the
context of cholecystitis, this is the keystone of BDI preven-
tion, and any failure to obtain CVS should lead to gradual
modifications of surgical strategy: calling upon a colleague
for assistance, performance of perioperative cholangiogra-
phy, choice of a ‘‘second option’’ technique: anterograde or
subtotal cholecystectomy, or conversion into laparotomy.
The role of perioperative cholangiography:
Notwithstanding sizable literature but with a very low level
of evidence, routine perioperative cholangiography during
cholecystectomy is not recommended. On the other hand,
in the when biliary anatomy is insufficiently ascertained, it
is recommended by the experts as a means of reducing the
risk of BDI, particularly in patients with acute cholecystitis
[42,43].
Alternative approaches in the event of
significant pedicular inflammation
When Calot’s triangle is severely inflammatory, or when dis-
section would entail a significant risk of bile duct injury, the
anterograde ‘‘dome-down’’ or ‘‘fundus-first’’ technique is
an alternative to the classic retrograde technique. Appli-
cation requires clear comprehension of the anatomy of the
cystic and hilar plates. Dissection must be maintained along
the subserosal internal layer so as to avoid any vascular
and/or bile duct lesion. If the subserosal internal layer
around the Calot triangle cannot be exposed, dissection
must not be further pursued, and subtotal cholecystectomy
may be preferred so as avoid vasculobiliary injury due to
surgical ‘‘error traps’’.
Subtotal cholecystectomy consists in opening the gall
bladder along its axis up to the collar and removing gall-
stones in such a way as to clearly identify the cystic duct.
Painstaking extraction of all gallstones is important in view
of avoiding any and all postoperative collection. Two types
of subtotal cholecystectomy have been described according
to closure or non-closure:
• in subtotal fenestrating cholecystectomy (no residual bile
duct remnant), the collar of the remaining gall bladder
remains open. This approach may entail a postoperative
biliary fistula, which is generally spontaneously resolved;
• in subtotal reconstituting cholecystectomy (existence of
gall bladder remnants), what remains of the gall bladder
is closed off by sutures.
Given the persistence of the remnants, this approach
can entail the formation of more gallstones and expose the
patient to the risk of recurrent cholecystitis necessitating a
potentially difficult but relatively rare (less than 5%) redo
operation. Performance of subtotal cholecystectomy must
be described in the operation report, and the patient has to
be informed of the fact that part of his or her gall bladder
has remained in place.
When they occur, bile duct injuries are often identified
preoperatively [47,48], occasionally permitting direct repair
by an experienced hepatobiliary surgeon in the absence of
any associated vascular lesion (10—20% of cases); such a
lesion can be responsible for secondary ischemic-type biliary
lesions, and compromise the repair. In all cases, it is recom-
mended that the surgeon be able to call upon a colleague for
assistance in the event of complex cholecystectomy, and, in
case of suspected BDI, to solicit the opinion of a specialized
team. Indeed, specialized management by a tertiary team
equipped with a surgical, radiological and endoscopic tech-
nical platform is associated with more satisfactory short and
long-term outcomes [42]. In clinical practice, topography of
the injury and associated vascular damage have an impact
on therapeutical attitude, which brings together specialized
endoscopic management and/or early or late surgical repair.
Conclusions
Rehospitalization after cholecystectomy is a frequent occur-
rence in numerous hospital units worldwide (up to 17%).
In France, while rate of rehospitalization has not been
closely studied, it has been reported as 7.5% in the specific
case of patients operated for acute cholecystitis [9]. The
main causes of rehospitalization are residual MBD gallstones
(20%), pain (16%), surgical site infections (14%), nau-
sea/vomiting (12%), and hemorrhagic complications (8%).
Much dreaded, bile duct injuries represent 0.008% of causes
for readmission. Prevention of rehospitalization necessitates
rigorous selection of surgical indications (based partially on
preoperative imagery), characterized by pre or periopera-
tive BDI exploration in case of doubt on the presence of a
gallstone in this area; information given to the patient and
his/her kith and kin; pre, peri and postoperative applica-
tion of protocols on surgical discomfort management and,
more generally and if possible, application of protocols on
enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS); performance of
surgery in compliance with several rules, including Critical
View of Safety certification (or, if necessary, performance
of anterograde or subtotal cholecystectomy); calling upon a

causes, which may range from the need for optimized medi-

Keypoints
• Rehospitalization rates after cholecystectomy range
worldwide from 0 to 11.7% according to series and
definitions. In France, rehospitalization rates are not
published on a nationwide scale, with the exception
of acute cholecystitis (7.5%).
• The causes of rehospitalization are dominated by
residual gallstones in the main bile duct (20%), pain
(16%) surgical site infections (14%), nausea/vomiting
(12%) and hemorrhagic complications (8%). Bile
duct injuries constitute 0.008% of the causes of
readmission.
• Among the surgical risk factors, acute cholecystitis
is clearly associated with excess morbidity and
increased risk of rehospitalization.
• Prevention of rehospitalization calls for:
◦ rigorous 	selection 	of 	the 	indications 	for
surgery, 	particularly 	including 	preoperative
or perioperative exploration of the primary gall
bladder in cases of suspected gallstone in that
area;
◦ informing the patient and his/her kith and kin;
◦ preoperative, perioperative and postoperative
application of the relevant surgical discomfort
management protocols and, more generally, if
possible, application of enhanced recovery after
surgery (ERAS) protocols;
◦ performance of an operation in full compliance
with several rules: acquisition of critical view of
safety assessment (or performance, if necessary,
of an anterograde or subtotal cholecystectomy),
calling a colleague when experiencing doubt on
the need for induction of perioperative bile duct
injury or request for advice from a specialized
center, ablation of the different gallbladder
stones, acquisition of satisfactory hemostasis,
non-contamination of the intestinal wall during
extraction of the surgical specimen.
• Routine performance during the cholecystectomy
of cholangiography in view of preventing the
risk of residual gallstone or bile duct injury is
not recommended. On the other hand, in case
of uncertainty on biliary anatomy, the experts
recommend cholangiography, especially in patients
with acute cholecystitis.
