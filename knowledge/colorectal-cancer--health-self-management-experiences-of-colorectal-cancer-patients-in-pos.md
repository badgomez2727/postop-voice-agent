A B S T R A C T
Purpose: Health self-management helps improve health-related quality of life and life satisfaction, as well as
cancer survival. The study aim was to explore the essence of the health self-management experiences and support
needs of colorectal cancer patients after surgeries.
Methods: The study was based on phenomenology research methodology. Purposive sampling was used to obtain
a heterogeneous sample to provide rich information regarding the research questions. Participants were recruited
from colorectal surgery outpatient clinics in a hospital in Taiwan. Data were collected by semi-structured in-
depth interviews and analyzed by thematic content analysis. Strategies adapted from Lincoln and Guba were
used to enhance the trustworthiness of the study.
Results: Ten participants, 5 males and 5 females, were interviewed. Their health self-management experience fell
into 3 overarching themes and 9 related subthemes. Our results show that (1) seeking support when experiencing
discomfort, (2) when life changes, re-adjust accordingly, and (3) staying positive and self-perseverance are the
essences of the health self-management experiences and support needs of postoperative colorectal cancer
patients.
Conclusions: Postoperative colorectal cancer patients experienced tremendous physical and psychosocial chal-
lenges after returning home from the hospital. Although burdened with multiple stressors, these patients were
able to seek support, learning to practice self-care, facing cancer positively, and exhibit positive growth in life.
Patients with colorectal cancer have to constantly adjust to the impacts of their diseases. The study results may
provide as a reference for supporting postoperative adjustment and promoting health self-management among
patients with colorectal cancer.
1. Introduction
Colorectal cancer was the third most common cancer worldwide
with approximately 1.8 million new cases in 2018 (Bray et al., 2018).
Surgery is the primary treatment for colorectal cancer (Dekker et al.,
2019; National Comprehensive Cancer Network, 2020). In Taiwan, 4520
adults were diagnosed with colorectal cancer in 2017. Most of them
(91.17%) received surgeries as their initial treatments (Taiwan Health
Promotion Administration, 2019). Postoperative patients often face
challenges brought on by their disease and treatments. These may
include managing physical discomfort and treatment adverse effects,
rebuilding daily routines and social roles, preventing disease recurrence,
and addressing subsequent psychological distress (Dekker et al., 2019;
Dunn et al., 2013a, 2013b; McDermott et al., 2015; Scheele et al., 2015).
Health self-management can be defined as the skills that individuals

includes health care management, daily life management, and
emotional stress management. Health self-management emphasizes
partnerships with health care providers to solve problems and perform
self-care (Lorig and Holmon, 2003). Good health self-management helps
improve health-related quality of life, life satisfaction, and cancer sur-

To date, there have been few studies on the self-management expe-
rience and needs of postoperative patients with colorectal cancer. Pan
et al. (2011) conducted a descriptive, longitudinal study (n = 35) to
investigate symptom distress and self-care strategies of postoperative
colorectal cancer patients with diarrhea. They found that patients rated
dietary modification and exercise as the most helpful self-care strategies.
In a cross-sectional survey (n = 175), Hou et al. (2017) also found that
diet self-management was the most common behavior in rectal cancer
patients after sphincter-preserving surgery. Most previous studies in
understating postoperative colorectal cancer patients’ experience have
been focused on ostomy associated experience or care needs (Cengiz and
Bahar, 2017; Herlufsen and Brodsgaard, 2017; Ceylan and Vural, 2017),
the life experience (Jakobsson et al., 2017; Jonsson et al., 2011; Worster
and Holmes, 2009), symptoms or distress (Abelson et al., 2018; Krogs-
gaard et al., 2014), or the significance of information (Lithner et al.,
2015; Samuelsson et al., 2018). Results of an integrative review showed
that patients with colorectal cancer faced various experiences during
postoperative recovery (Changyai et al., 2020). Suffering, decreased
activity, ambivalent feelings, and the need for caring were the main
themes of their experiences (Changyai et al., 2020). Only one qualitative
study investigated the self-management strategies used by colorectal
cancer patients to handle the complicated circumstances within the care
of their diseases (Ose et al., 2017). The results showed that “proactive
demanding,” “proactive behavior,” and “proactive data management”
were self-management strategies used by patients to handle the
complexity of their situations (Ose et al., 2017). However, these authors
did specify whether the interviewed patients were in postoperative re-
covery or other stages of cancer treatment trajectories. Besides, the
study findings were generated from focus groups of colorectal cancer
patients, representatives of support groups, physicians, and other health
care professionals. The results might be different from solely patients’
perspectives or inputs.
Therefore, this study uses a phenomenological approach to explore
the essence of the health self-management experiences and support
needs of colorectal cancer patients during the transition to home after
colorectal cancer surgery. Phenomenology research focuses on in-
dividuals’ experiences of phenomena and how they interpret these ex-
periences (Douglass and Moustakas 1985). For the study purpose, a
phenomenological methodology is considered a suitable approach.
2. Methods
2.1. Study design
This is a phenomenology research. Data were collected by semi-
structured in-depth interviews and analyzed by thematic content
analysis.
2.2. Participants recruitment
Purposive sampling was used to obtain a heterogeneous sample to
explore how health self-management is experienced among different
patients and provide rich information regarding the research questions.
Participants were recruited from colorectal surgery outpatient clinics in
a hospital in Taiwan. Potentially eligible patients were referred by
surgeons and approached by one of the researchers (P.-S. L.). Patients
who met the following eligibility criteria and could most usefully inform
the study and maximize the diversity relevant to the research questions
were invited to participate. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) aged
20 and older, (2) a confirmed diagnosis of stage I–III colorectal cancer,
(3) received tumor resection surgeries in the previous month, and (4)
able to speak Mandarin or Taiwanese. Exclusion criteria were as follows:
(1) diagnosed with a terminal illness, (2) having a severe mental illness,
(3) receiving chemotherapy or radiation therapy pre-operatively, and
(4) having a colostomy. The theoretical saturation was used as the cri-
terion for stopping recruitment (Glaser and Strauss, 1967).
2.3. Ethical considerations
The study was approved by the Mackay Memorial Hospital Institu-
tional Review Board (IRB No. 17MMHIS062). One of the researchers (P.-
S. L.) explained the study purpose, the interview process, and the tape-
recording to the participants. The researcher also assured participants
that their data would only be used for research purposes and remained
confidential. They could stop the interview or withdraw from the study
at any time without penalty or affecting their care. If the interview had
caused emotional stress to them, the interviewer would have referred
them to the outpatient clinic for assessment and treatment. All partici-
pants signed informed consent before the interviews took place. There
was neither withdrawal from the study nor adverse reactions to the
interviewing occurred.
2.4. Data collection
Data were collected through in-depth interviews by a nursing
researcher (P.-S. L.) trained in qualitative methodology. After obtaining
a participant’s informed consent, the nursing researcher interviewed the
participant in a quiet and undisturbed room in the clinic or the partic-
ipant’s home. Data were generated through semi-structured interviews
using a topic guide. Interview questions prompted participants to share
their experiences, and the interview process also allowed them to ex-
press their views and tell their stories. Could you please talk about your
experience after returning home from the hospital? How does colorectal
cancer affect your life in general? How do you cope with your condi-
tions? What kinds of help have you received or would like to receive
from your health care providers? Each interview took about 1–1.5 h and
was tape-recorded. The interviewer recorded the interview field notes
and retrieved the participants’ disease and treatment information from
their medical records. All participants were one-to-one interviewed
except for one who was interviewed with his wife present.
2.5. Data analysis
Data collection and analysis were carried out simultaneously. Data
were analyzed using thematic content analyses. The essence of patients’
self-management experience and support needs was explored by un-
derstanding and interpreting interview data. The content analysis
method is based on existing theoretical knowledge and interprets the
data’s potential meaning. It can use the categories or themes suggested
by a theory to analyze the data in a deductive way. It is also possible to
classify data-derived categories or topics when knowledge related to the
phenomenon is limited (Hsieh and Shannon, 2005). This study utilized
the latter.
After each interview, the recordings were transcribed verbatim and
then analyzed. First, two nursing researchers (P.-S. L. and Y.-P. L.)
carefully read the verbatim manuscript to understand the interviewee’s
experience and the overall context and extracted the meaning units for
preliminary coding. They discussed and finalized the code list. Next, the
two researchers coded the remaining transcripts and organized codes
into categories. Third, they organized categories into sub-themes and
then organized sub-themes into overarching themes (Table 2). During

2.6. Rigor of the study
Strategies adapted from Lincoln and Guba (1986) and Forero et al.
(2018) were used to enhance the trustworthiness of the study in
four-dimension criteria (credibility, dependability, confirmability, and
transferability). To achieve credibility, we 1) built the research team
with all investigators furnished adequate knowledge and skills to
accomplish their roles, 2) ensured the interviewer spending time to
engage with participants before their interviews, 3) tested the interview
protocol with two pilot interviews to finalize the interviewing process
and techniques, 4) kept the interview field notes and analyzed them with
the interview data, and 5) implemented monthly debriefing sessions
with physicians and nurse practitioners of the colorectal surgical
department. To achieve dependability, we 1) developed data collection
guidelines, 2) kept a detailed record of the data collection process, 3)
checked coding accuracy by a second coder to ensure high levels of
consistency, and 4) retained the verbatim transcripts and the notes taken
during the interviews to allow future verification. To achieve confirm-
ability, the interviewer wrote reflexive journals and discussed it with
other researchers to exclude prejudice and bias from an objective and
neutral perspective during biweekly team meetings. To achieve trans-
ferability, we used a purposive sampling technique to form a designated
sample and operationalized data saturation criterion for ceasing
recruitment.
3. Results
3.1. Characteristics of participants
Ten participants, five males, and five females were interviewed.
Their ages ranged between 44 and 76. The tumor sites included the
descending colon, ascending colon, sigmoid colon, cecum, and rectum.
The pathological cancer stages ranged from stage I to IIIb (Table 1).
Their health self-management experience fell into three overarching
themes and nine related subthemes (Table 2).
3.2. Health self-management experience
3.2.1. Theme one: seeking support when experiencing discomfort
This refers to the process of trying new methods, keeping symptoms
under control, coping with the treatment, and seeking resources for self-
care when experiencing physical discomfort. It includes three sub-
themes: “attempting to control physical discomfort”, “trusting physician
and cooperating with the treatment plans”, and “searching for infor-
mation and taking care of oneself”.
“Attempting to control physical discomfort” refers to the process
of attempting to control and improve symptoms when experiencing
physical discomfort after the surgical treatment. Early in their return,
patients often experienced symptoms such as diarrhea, abdominal
distension, frequent bowel movements, and loose stool. They tried to
develop self-care strategies to improve these symptoms, such as eating
smaller meals, walking, having a low fiber diet, and taking prescribed
medications. Most of their symptoms gradually improved over time.

3.2.2. Theme two: when life changes, re-adjust accordingly
A cancer diagnosis is a huge shock as well as a turning point in life.
Patients realize the importance of their health. They review their past
lifestyles and try to establish a healthier diet and exercise routines. They
also learn about self-care so that their health can be soon recovered and
not cause too much burden to their family. It includes three sub-themes:
“cancer changes one’s life and reminding one to pay attention to one’s
health”, “reflecting on oneself and changing one’s lifestyle”, and “taking
care of oneself and cherishing one’s family”.
“Cancer changing one’s life and reminding one to pay attention
to one’s health” refers to having experienced the threat of cancer poses
to life, one realizes the importance of maintaining health. Participants
expressed how their cancer diagnosis made them realize the importance
of their health and how it caused them to reexamine their previous lack
of attention to their health, giving them motives to maintain their
health.
“Initially, it was a huge shock. I wondered why I was this unlucky … This
was a turning point in my life, and I reexamined my health … This is what
has happened; you should now learn to cherish yourself and your health”
(Case 1). “I felt that my situation was caused by not taking care of my
health” (Case 2). “I am still quite young; I don’t want to be bedbound …
Now I am focused on regaining my health” (Case 7). “One must maintain
one’s health and not rely too much on others … We cannot schedule ill-
nesses and prepare for them in advance” (Case 10).
“Reflecting on oneself and changing one’s lifestyles” refers to
reexamining how one’s lifestyle has been less than ideal and trying to
establish a healthier lifestyle. Most participants reported reviewing their
unhealthy diets and lifestyle habits and noted that they required self-
discipline and a healthier lifestyle. This would involve diet, exercise,
smoking, alcohol, and stress from work.
“After I was diagnosed with colon cancer, I felt that I should have led a
more balanced lifestyle so that this would not have happened” (Case 2).
“Because of my work, I often ate out. This might have contributed to my
tumor” (Case 6). “After the surgery, I had to cooperate with the treatment
plan and change my old habits. If I did not change them, my body would
be like it had been … Now I don’t drink or smoke anymore and eat three
regular meals” (Case 2). “When I hear that some food will help, or some
doctor will help, I pursue these options to my utmost” (Case 10). “One
must think of ways to change bad habits … I need to remember to eat
healthier, exercise regularly, and find ways to relieve pressure …” (Case
3). “I need to force myself to relax. Sometimes I get too anxious, sleep too
late, and that needs to be changed …” (Case 10). “Living is more
important; it’s alright if work performance suffers. Before, I would ask
more of myself and put a lot of pressure on myself, hoping to make
everything perfect” (Case 3). From now on, I know that I need to get
regular checkups because prevention is far better than treatment” (Case
1).

learning to care for oneself and not creating a burden for one’s family,
and recognizing and cherishing the care received from one’s family.
Most participants tried to take care of themselves to prevent burdening
their families. Their family and friends’ love and support made these
patients more motivated to take care of themselves. They tried not to
make their family worry and not to be a burden on them.

4. Discussion
Our results show that (1) seeking support when experiencing
discomfort, (2) when life changes, re-adjust accordingly, and (3) staying
positive and self-perseverance are the essences of the health self-
management experiences and support needs of postoperative colo-
rectal cancer patients.
4.1. Seeking support when experiencing discomfort
Similar to previous studies’ findings (Dekker et al., 2019; Hilde-
brandt et al., 2019), diarrhea and bloating were common symptoms
experienced in these postoperative colorectal cancer patients. In
attempting to control physical discomfort, these participants tried to eat
small meals and make dietary adjustments, as reported in previous
studies. However, the physical symptoms can still adversely affect an
individual’s daily life (Russell et al., 2015). Some participants reported
troubled by frequent bowel movements and were reluctant to go out
because of the inconvenience and the embarrassment of frequently
needing a toilet. As for the subtheme, trusting physicians and cooper-
ating with the treatment, participants indicated that they rely on phy-
sicians’ professional judgment while facing cancer diagnosis challenges
and the uncertainty of its treatment outcomes. Similar to what was re-
ported in a previous study (Lam et al., 2014), patients felt better pre-
pared psychologically. They were more able to actively cooperate with
their treatment plans after discussing with their physicians and
receiving medical professionals’ explanations. They try to follow their
physicians’ suggestions and cooperate fully with the treatment plans in
the hopes of recovering. Participants realized the importance of health
after been diagnosed with cancer and searched for self-care information
to manage their conditions. Some participants felt that the health in-
formation provided by their medical teams was not sufficient and
wished for more precise information on dietary and follow-up sugges-
tions. As suggested in previous studies, patients reported great needs for
self-care information after cancer treatment and hoped to receive rele-
vant information from their medical teams (Hoekstra et al., 2014).
4.2. When life changes, re-adjust accordingly
Participants perceived the threat of cancer to their life and realized
the importance of marinating health as the subtheme, cancer changes
one’s life and causes one to face one’s health. Participants reexamined
their health practice and tried to maintain their health better. Similar to
what was reported in previous studies (Hildebrandt et al., 2019;
McLachlan et al., 2015), a cancer diagnosis was seen as a warning for an
individual to pay more attention to their health. Relevant education and
support measures can be used to enhancing patients’ self-care during
this critical period. As for the subtheme, reflecting on oneself and
changing one’s lifestyle, participants thought of their previous un-
healthy lifestyles as the cause of their cancer. They tried to maintain a
healthy lifestyle to decrease the risk of recurrence. Participants reported
searching for information about food choices, physical activity, and
dietary supplements. Similar to findings in previous studies, cancer
patients often linked the cause of cancer with their unhealthy behavior
and decided to work hard at changing their lifestyles (Hildebrandt et al.,
2019; Kanera et al., 2016; McLachlan et al., 2015). Rock et al. (2012)
also reported that cancer survivors often seek self-care strategies to
improve their long-term outcomes, reduce recurrence risk, and improve
quality of life. This could be treated as a window of opportunity to help
patients build a healthy lifestyle. As for the subtheme, taking care of
oneself and cherishing one’s family, participants experienced the care
that their family provided during their treatment and cherished their
feelings and connections with their families. Participants expressed a
strong desire to recover quickly through self-care as a way to repay their
family. Similar to what was reported in a qualitative study (Hildebrandt
et al., 2019; Law et al., 2018), perceived support from family and
important others affirmed the value of life and enhanced patients’
cooperation with the treatment plans. This gratefulness is also a moti-
vator for adopting a healthy lifestyle.
4.3. Stay positive and genuine to yourself
After surgical resection, patients were troubled by symptoms and
under great emotional and psychological stress. They must face their

situations with a positive attitude and coping strategies. Therefore, the
participants reported the experience of adjusting one’s perspective and
facing the crisis bravely. As findings in previous studies, the study par-
ticipants said that positivity and optimistic attitudes help them meet the
psychological challenges of colorectal cancer (Dunn et al., 2013b; Seiler
and Jenewein, 2019). These findings suggest the importance of resil-
ience for psychological adjustment in this population because resilience
is the ability to show positive emotions, such as hope, optimism, and
happiness, and change the crisis into a turning point (Seiler and Jene-
wein, 2019). As for the subtheme, building strength and confidence, the
participants were able to face cancer courageously with the help of
religious beliefs, support from family and friends, and the sharing of
experience among patients. Similar to previous studies’ findings, reli-
gious belief and support systems helped patients face the psychosocial
challenges of cancer (Chang et al., 2016; Law et al., 2018; Saraf et al.,
2013; Whitford and Olver, 2012). The study findings also support that
family is a core support group and plays a vital role in supporting the
psychological adjustment of cancer patients (Law et al., 2018). As for the
subtheme, comprehending life, to learn and to grow, the life-threatening
experience of having cancer remind a patient to think about the value of
life. Some participants took it as an opportunity to re-plan their life and
restart again. Some were more involved in religion. Others appreciated
their family and friends’ support and hoped that they could help those in
need afterward. Similar to previous studies’ findings (Saraf et al., 2013;
Whitford and Olver, 2012), cancer patients might redefine their mean-
ing of life and become more active in their beliefs. These may enhance
their spiritual growth and inspire them to care for others, thereby
improving their life value.
4.4. Limitations and recommendations for future research
There are several limitations to this study. First, we recruited only
patients who received tumor resection surgeries in the previous month.
These patients can only provide acute postoperative self-management
experience, not the complete picture of self-management experience.
In particular, all patients in this stage had not yet received any adjuvant
chemotherapy or radiotherapy, which was usually started later on. Pa-
tients might require different self-management strategies during
chemotherapy or radiotherapy. Second, we excluded patients receiving
chemotherapy or radiation therapy pre-operatively, or with a co-
lostomy; therefore, these patients’ experience was not included in the
current study. Third, interviews took place when participants returned
to the hospital for a follow-up appointment and mostly occurred in the
hospital’s prayer room. The timing and locations might not be ideal for
interviewing and may impede participants from expressing themselves
fully. Moreover, the presence of a recording or a family member may
also hinder the depth of interviews. Two participants expressed their
concerns regarding the tape recording and would only speak more freely
after the interviewer promised to maintain their confidentiality. One
participant was interviewed with his wife present. Nevertheless, the
study results help us to understand the health self-management experi-
ence of colorectal cancer surgical patients.
Future studies exploring patients’ self-management experience
receiving chemotherapy or radiotherapy, or those with a colostomy, can
advance our knowledge in these patients. They might have different
concerns and need a different set of self-management skills and support.
A longer duration follow-up is suggested for understanding changes in
patients’ self-management experience overtime. One may also consider
using the grounded theory approach to explore the social interaction
and process of developing self-management competency after colorectal
cancer surgery.
4.5. Relevance to clinical practice
The study findings are relevant to understanding patients’ health
self-management experience during the transition to home after
colorectal cancer surgery. The patients indicated a strong desire for in-
formation on dietary choices, follow-up methods, and physical strength
maintenance during chemotherapy. However, most patients reported
that nurses’ care information and the content of the health education
manual were not specific enough. These findings suggest areas for nurses
to improve and advance their practice. Although the period in the hos-
pital may be very brief for these patients, nurses can provide tailored
discharge instructions that address self-management of physical symp-
toms and emotional distress. Nurses or nurse practitioners in the
outpatient area, who at the first follow-up visit, should assess patients
for issues and intervene appropriately. Furthermore, patients reported
that the support of medical personnel, religion, family and friends, and
fellow patients helped them stay positive and be confident that they
could overcome cancer. Cancer care is multidimensional and holistic.
Nurses should develop a set of plans by which patients receive complete
medical care and support and assistance from professionals and family
members as their treatment progresses to help patients face cancer
challenges. The study results provide a reference for nurses to support
postoperative adjustment and promote health self-management among
patients with colorectal cancer.
5. Conclusion
The immediate transition to home after colorectal cancer surgery
presents the patient and family with multiple physical and psychological
challenges and requires a shift in self-management strategies. This
phenomenological research explored the essence of patients’ health self-
management experience during this critical transition. The results
showed that patients would “seek support when experiencing discom-
fort,” searching for resources and cooperating with their treatment.
They also felt the threat that cancer posed to their lives and its effect on
their health. They realized that “when life changes, re-adjust accord-
ingly,” and reviewed their past behaviors as well as appreciated the care
provided by their family. Despite the shock of their diagnosis, they felt
supported and tried to “stay positive and true to themselves,” searching
for coping mechanisms and appreciating the value of life. Patients with
colorectal cancer have to adjust to the impacts of their diseases and
treatments constantly. Although burdened with multiple stressors, pa-
tients could seek support, learn to practice self-care, face cancer posi-
tively, and exhibit positive growth in life.
