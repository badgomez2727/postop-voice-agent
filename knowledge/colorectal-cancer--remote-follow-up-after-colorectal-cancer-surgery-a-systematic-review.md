ABSTRACT
Background: Remote consultations are increasingly being utilized in colorectal cancer follow-up, especially since the COVID-19
pandemic, with benefits continuing beyond the pandemic. It is not clear how remote modalities affect patient satisfaction, quality
of life (QoL) and safety of colorectal cancer follow-up.
Objectives: To investigate the impact of remote follow-up on patient satisfaction, QoL, clinician satisfaction, adherence to inves-
tigations, readmission rates, recurrence rates, and mortality rates for colorectal cancer.
Methods: A systematic review was performed using three electronic databases: MEDLINE, EMBASE, and Cochrane Central
Register of Controlled Trials, along with grey literature. Eligible studies included remote techniques such as telephone, video,
or patient-initiated follow-up (PIFU) compared with standard face-to-face follow-up in patients who have undergone colorectal
cancer resection. Two independent reviewers screened studies and assessed the risk of bias.
Results: A total of 4417 records were identified with seven studies included. Six studies showed high levels of patient satisfaction
in remote follow-up, with two studies showing significantly better satisfaction than standard follow-up. Two studies evaluated
QoL, with one showing significantly better QoL in the remote follow-up group than the standard, and the other study showing
comparable levels. Safety outcomes were minimally reported, but there were no adverse outcomes. There was at least moderate
risk of bias in the four nonrandomized interventions due to confounding from patient selection into intervention groups.
Conclusions: Remote follow-up in colorectal cancer appears to be comparable to face-to-face follow-up with high levels of
patient satisfaction and QoL. While adherence to follow-up investigations was not compromised, the evidence on oncological
impact is limited, and further studies on mortality and recurrence rates are required to ensure overall safety.
1 | Background
Remote consultations in clinical cancer practice are a rela-
tively novel innovation that has now been instituted worldwide.
Modalities include telephone, video, and patient-initiated fol-
low-up (PIFU). In PIFU, patients do not have scheduled follow-up
visits but still have regular follow-up investigations organized by
their healthcare provider and can contact their provider for an ap-
pointment if they develop symptoms. Since the onset of COVID-19,
the uptake of telehealth and virtual follow-ups has skyrocketed,
providing many benefits to patients [1–3]. Benefits include the con-
venience of accessing a specialist from home or work, minimizing
travel times and costs, minimizing time away from work and time
in the waiting room, and facilitating easier consultations for dis-
abled patients or those less mobile. Over 40% of the world's popu-
lation lives rurally [4], therefore remote consultations may reduce
geographical inequities in accessing healthcare. In addition, the
use of remote follow-up can reduce the impact on hospital service
use, allowing increased capacity for new patients on the waiting
list to be seen in a timelier manner [5].

Colorectal cancer has a significant burden on healthcare, being
the third most common cancer in the world [6]. There are pa-
tient needs specific to colorectal cancer that may not pertain to
other cancer types, such as stoma care, body image, and sexual
function, which may have a significant impact on quality of life
(QoL) and psychological morbidity [7–9]. It is unclear what im-
pact remote follow-up in colorectal cancer may have on patient
satisfaction and QoL.
A preliminary scoping literature review identified several stud-
ies, including randomized trials, that have examined the use
of virtual follow-up in colorectal cancer [10–13]. Patient satis-
faction and QoL are the main outcomes evaluated, with either
equivocal or slightly favorable outcomes for remote follow-up.
The clinician perspective has been generally in favor of remote
follow-up. The safety of this intervention has been evaluated to
a varying degree in studies, with outcomes that include adher-
ence to follow-up investigations, unplanned readmissions, re-
currence rate, and mortality. From the limited reports, there is
no negative impact on safety.
General literature reviews have examined remote follow-up in
colorectal cancer [14, 15], and systematic reviews of remote
follow-up in cancer have been published [16, 17], however cur-
rently, there is no systematic review that has evaluated the use
of remote follow-up in colorectal cancer. The aim of this review
was to determine whether remote follow-up is equally accept-
able as traditional face- to-face outpatient follow-up for patients
who have undergone colorectal cancer resection. The primary
outcome was patient satisfaction. Secondary outcomes included
QoL, clinician satisfaction, and safety aspects such as adherence
to investigations, readmission rate, recurrence rates, and mortal-
ity rates.
2 | Methods
This systematic review was completed using the Preferred
Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses
(PRISMA) guidelines [18]. A protocol was registered with
PROSPERO (CRD42024563412) [19].
2.1 | Search Strategy
Databases searched included PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane
Central Register of Controlled Trials.
Records were identified through trials registries: https://www.
clinicaltrials.gov, https://trialsearch.who.int, https://www.clini
caltrialsregister.eu, and https://www.anzctr.org.au.
Grey literature was searched using conference proceed-
ings, Google, Google Scholar, and reference lists of relevant
articles. All databases were searched from inception until
July 2024.
Search terms included the following: cancer, colorectal, bowel,
colon, rectal, remote, telephone, video, virtual, nurse-led, fol-
low-up. There were no filters or limits, and there was no restric-
tion on date of publication.
2.1.1 | Inclusion Criteria
– Population:
○ Adults over the age of 16 with colorectal cancer.
○ Postoperative surgical resection for nonmetastatic col-
orectal cancer.
– Intervention:
○ Participants were involved in remote follow-up methods
such as phone call, video conference, or PIFU.
– Comparison:
○ Traditional outpatient face-to-face follow- up.
– Outcomes:
○ Patient satisfaction, QoL, clinician satisfaction, adher-
ence to investigations, readmission rate, recurrence rate,
and mortality rate.
– Studies:
○ Randomized controlled trials (RCT) and nonran-
domized intervention trials, including retrospective
studies.
○ English articles or translation available.
2.1.2 | Exclusion Criteria
– Review articles, qualitative studies, meta-analyses, case
studies.
2.1.3 | Study Selection
The titles and abstracts of the initial search results were screened
independently by G.S. and C.F. Relevant articles underwent in-
dependent full text review. Any discrepancies were discussed
and resolved by the senior author C.H.
2.2 | Data Extraction and Synthesis
Data were collected from relevant articles using a spread-
sheet. Information collected included author, journal, pub-
lication year, outcomes assessed, study design, sample size,
and method of remote follow-up. Clinical outcomes included
patient satisfaction, QoL, clinician satisfaction, adherence to
follow-up investigations, recurrence rates, readmission rates,
and mortality.
Dichotomous outcomes and rates were described using fre-
quency and percentages. Continuous outcomes were de-
scribed as mean and 95% confidence intervals (CI), or median
and interquartile ranges (IQR). Meta-analysis was not possi-
ble due to significant heterogeneity of interventions and out-
comes. The results have been described through a qualitative
synthesis.
2.3 | Quality Assessment
The risk of bias was assessed using the RoB 2 tool [20] in RCTs
and ROBINS-I [21] for nonrandomized trials. This was carried

out by two independent reviewers, G.S. and C.F., with consensus
discussion.
3 | Results
A total of 4417 records from databases and registries were identi-
fied through the search strategy, of which five met our inclusion
criteria, while two additional reports were identified through a
grey literature search (Figure 1). There were three randomized
controlled studies and four nonrandomized interventional stud-
ies. See Table 1 for a summary of individual studies.
3.1 | Quality Appraisal
The main risk of bias was due to confounding from selection of
patients into intervention and comparison groups in the non-
randomized studies. The different cohorts of patients would
likely have impacted the outcomes due to various baseline,
clinical, and psychosocial factors. The RCTs were judged low
risk of bias [22, 23] and some concerns [13] overall. See Table 2
for a summary of the risk of bias.
3.2 | Patient Satisfaction
Six studies included data on patient satisfaction; however, there
was high variability in the tools utilized. Three studies utilized
validated 5-point Likert scales for outcome measures [12, 13, 22].
One RCT of 336 patients evaluating PIFU utilized Likert scales,
dichotomised for analysis into “sufficient/almost sufficient” ver-
sus “not sufficient/no information” for the first three items, and
“high/some degree” versus “insufficient/no help” for the last
two items. The intervention group had approximately 90% of
participants scoring highly for satisfaction in all five questions,
compared to approximately 80% in the control group (p-values
were 0.04 or less for all five questions) [22].
Another RCT investigating 25 patients undergoing telephone
follow-up compared to 25 undergoing hospital follow-up re-
ported a higher mean score for satisfaction with the most re-
cent appointment in the intervention group (mean: 9.8, 95% CI:
9.6–10.0, Cohen's d = 0.45) compared to the control group (mean:
9.5, 95% CI: 9.2–9.8). The unadjusted score was not significant
(p = 0.104), when adjusted for baseline scores, the intervention
group had higher satisfaction (p = 0.029) [13].

The third validated Likert scale was a 10-item Multi- Source
Feedback tool used in a nonrandomized study comparing
video consultations with face-to-face follow-up. There was a
high level of satisfaction in both methods, with differences
observed in favor of video consultations but not statistically
significant [12].
One study of PIFU designed a tool specifically for the study,
which included rating scores from 0 to 100 for quality of care,
a five-point Likert scale for expectations, and yes/no/unsure
for acceptability. It showed no significant difference in patients'
views of quality and expectations; however, it found that PIFU
had higher acceptability (97.3%, n = 36) than standard follow-up
(75%, n = 24; p = 0.01) [24].
The remaining two studies utilized only yes/no questions in
the measurement of patient satisfaction. One study showed
high levels in both intervention (100%, n = 52) and control
groups (98%, n = 61) [25]. The other study only measured
patient satisfaction in the intervention group, with 100% re-
sponding yes to satisfaction with the surveillance method.
Only two out of 22 patients desired more personal contact
with a health professional [26].
3.3 | Quality of Life
Two studies reported QoL or psychological morbidity [13, 24].
Health-related QoL was measured in one study of PIFU using
the validated EuroQol Group questionnaire (EQ-5D-5L) and
Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy (FACT) question-
naires. QoL was statistically significantly better for the inter-
vention group compared to the control group after 8 months (see
Table S1).
The other study, an RCT of telephone follow-up, measured psy-
chological morbidity using the validated State–Trait Anxiety
Inventory (STAI) and the General Health Questionnaire (GHQ).
While mean scores for STAI were higher in the control group
(Cohen's d = 0.66), the difference was not significant (p = 0.297).
There was no difference in GHQ scores between intervention
and standard groups (p = 0.626) (see Table S1).
3.4 | Clinician Satisfaction
Two studies reported clinician satisfaction. One study utilized a
10-item questionnaire with five 5-point Likert scales, four yes/
no questions, and one open-ended question, and found a higher
level of health provider satisfaction with video consultation (me-
dian: 9, IQR: 9–10) compared to standard face-to-face follow-up
(median: 8, IQR: 7–8) [12]. The other study only measured cli-
nician satisfaction for the intervention utilizing interviews and
reported health personnel were satisfied; however, no measures
were reported [12, 26].
3.5 | Adherence to Follow-Up Investigations
Two studies measured the adherence to follow-up investiga-
tions. One RCT of telephone follow-up found a reduced number
of computed tomography (CT) scans requested for the interven-
tion group (10/25) compared to control (14/25) although this
was not significant. There was no difference in the frequency of
blood tests or colonoscopies [13].
The other study of telephone follow-up reported more 6-monthly
carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) blood tests in the intervention
group (88.5%, n = 46) compared to the control (57.6%, n = 59,
p < 0.001). There were similar proportions of CT scans (100%
vs. 91.4%, p = 0.69) and colonoscopies (93.9% vs. 93.6%, p = 1.00)
completed in both groups [13, 25]. See Table 3 for a comparison
between studies.
3.6 | Readmission Rate
Three studies reported on acute hospital utilization (see Table 3).
One RCT reported 19 (11%) patients were readmitted at least
once in the intervention group compared to 27 (16%) in the con-
trol group (p = 0.30) [13]. Another study had three (12%) patients
from the intervention group readmitted compared to one (4%)
from the standard group [21]. The third study did not show data
but reported no significant difference in hospital utilization [23].
3.7 | Recurrence Rate
Four studies reported recurrence rates for both intervention and
standard follow-up groups (see Table 3). One RCT reported sim-
ilar 5-year recurrences in each group with 18 (33%) in the inter-
vention group and 17 (32%) in control [22]. Another RCT had one
recurrence in the intervention group (4%, n = 25), compared to
two in the standard group (8%, n = 25) [13]. The mean follow-up
time was not specified; however, the study stated their hospital's
usual colorectal cancer surveillance follow-up time is 5 years.
Another study reported a recurrence rate of 7.7% (n = 4/52) in the
intervention group over a median follow-up of 26.5 months com-
pared to 17.7% (n = 11/62) over a median follow-up of 15 months
in the standard group, but this difference was not significant
(p = 0.10) [25]. The fourth study reported no recurrences in ei-
ther group over 2 years of follow-up [24].
3.8 | Mortality
Two studies reported mortality data for each group (see Table 3)
[13, 23]. One study had 18 (33%) deaths out of 54 patients after
5 years of follow-up compared with the control group, which
had 13 (25%) deaths out of 53 patients (p = 0.264) [22]. The other
study had one (4%) death in the standard follow-up group of 25
patients and none in the intervention group [13].
4 | Discussion
This is the first systematic review examining the impact of re-
mote follow-up strategies on various patient and clinical out-
comes specifically for patients who have undergone colorectal
cancer surgery. A total of seven studies, including three RCTs,
were included. Modalities included video, nurse-led tele-
phone, and PIFU. Remote follow-up is a feasible alternative to

traditional face-to-face follow-up, with high levels of patient
satisfaction and QoL; however, there was limited evidence on
oncological outcomes to ensure its overall safety.
4.1 | Patient Satisfaction
Six studies measured patient satisfaction, with diverse outcome
measures. Three studies used validated Likert tools; however,
there was significant variation between these, limiting the
ability to perform a meta-analysis. All six studies showed high
levels of patient satisfaction in both intervention and compar-
ison groups, with two RCTs showing statistically significantly
higher levels of patient satisfaction in the intervention groups.
There is confounding from patient selection into the interven-
tion group in the four nonrandomized studies, likely causing
bias to favor the intervention group. Another source of con-
founding and heterogeneity is the use of nurse-led follow-up.
Most included studies have used nurse-led follow-up in the
intervention group, with evidence showing benefits in patient
satisfaction from nurse-led follow-up [27, 28]. Audits of re-
mote follow-up in colorectal cancer patients, not included in
the review due to lack of comparison group, have shown high
levels of patient satisfaction [11, 29]. A systematic review by
Xiao et al. assessed different types of cancer patients under-
going telehealth follow-up, showing high levels of patient sat-
isfaction among patients with no difference between groups
[16]. Similarly, a systematic review by Newton et al. evaluating
PIFU in oncology patients, which included breast, prostate,
and colorectal patients, also showed no significant difference
in patient satisfaction [17]. These findings support the grow-
ing evidence suggesting that alternative follow-up strategies
are well received by cancer patients. However, there is a need
to standardize measurement tools for future research to en-
able comparisons and meta-analysis to be performed.
4.2 | Quality of Life
Two studies evaluated QoL, with one reporting statistically sig-
nificantly better health-related QoL using validated question-
naires (EQ-5D-5L and FACT), while the other study found no
significant difference in psychological morbidity using the STAI
and GHQ. These findings are similar to other studies on QoL
and suggest that remote follow-up patients have no significant
reduction in QoL [10, 29]. The systematic reviews by Xiao et al.
and Newton et al. showed comparable QoL in both intervention
and comparison groups [16, 17]. This is promising for imple-
menting remote follow-up at institutions established in face-to-
face follow-up where concerns regarding impaired patient QoL
could be alleviated.
4.3 | Clinician Satisfaction
There is limited research on clinician satisfaction, with our
review only having two studies assessing this. One study
showed higher clinician satisfaction with video consultations
compared to standard face-to-face follow-up, which is simi-
lar to literature on other cancer patients. An RCT evaluating
video compared to standard follow-up after prostatectomy
showed equally high clinician satisfaction [30]. A survey of
202 multidisciplinary cancer care clinicians found that the
majority were satisfied with telehealth, with many wanting to
increase its use [31].

a similar number of CT scans and colonoscopies requested in

data may be available on these outcomes soon. A Cochrane
meta-analysis by Jeffrey et al. evaluated intensive follow-up ver-
sus minimal follow-up in colorectal cancer with findings of no
significant difference in overall survival [32]. However, more
data specifically evaluating the oncological safety of remote fol-
low-up is needed.
4.6 | Limitations
This review was limited by the small number of studies and only
975 participants. Larger interventional studies in Europe are cur-
rently underway and recruiting participants evaluating PIFU,
such as the RESPONSE and PROMISE trials [33, 34], which will
add to the current evidence of remote follow-up in colorectal
cancer. In addition, this review had significant variability of in-
terventions and outcome measures, limiting the ability to per-
form a meta-analysis. In contrast to other systematic reviews of
remote follow-up [16, 17], the assessment of only colorectal can-
cer patients reduces the variability of comparisons due to disease
factors. Due to patient self-selection into intervention and com-
parison groups, confounding is a major factor in the four non-
randomized intervention studies. However, in real-world clinical
scenarios, it is feasible for patients to self-select their modality of
follow-up, as has been done in the nonrandomized studies.
5 | Conclusion
This systematic review suggests that remote follow-up after col-
orectal cancer surgery is comparable to face-to-face follow-up,
with a high level of patient satisfaction and QoL. While adher-
ence to investigations was not compromised, the evidence on on-
cological outcomes is limited. Further studies of mortality and
recurrence rates are required to ensure the overall safety of this
follow- up strategy.
