minute via nasal cannula, ensured

patent, and began a rapid infu-

ordered. A stat lactate level was also
obtained. Serum lactate is a critical
marker of tissue hypoperfusion
and sepsis severity. Elevated levels
indicate impaired oxygen delivery
at the cellular level, even before
profound hypotension develops. The
Surviving Sepsis Campaign (2021)

in all patients with suspected sepsis
or septic shock, as it guides both ini-
tial risk stratification and ongoing
treatment response.3 The patient’s

supporting the clinical decision of
early sepsis. 2 The nurse’s recogni-
tion of sepsis warning signs and
prompt action ensured this test was
obtained without delay, which con-
tributed to preventing progression
to septic shock.
The patient remained in the ED
for approximately 3.5 hours, during
which she received IV antibiot-
ics, ceftriaxone, and clindamycin,
prior to surgery. This combination
was selected because appendicitis
with localized abscess formation
is typically polymicrobial, involv-
ing Gram-negative bacilli (such as
Escherichia coli and Klebsiella) and
anaerobic organisms (such as
Bacteroides fragilis).4,5 Ceftriaxone, a
third-generation cephalosporin, of-
fers broad Gram-negative coverage
and excellent intra-abdominal
penetration, while clindamycin
provides robust anaerobic cover-
age. Used together, these agents
provide comprehensive periopera-
tive prophylaxis, reducing the risk
of postoperative intra-abdominal
abscesses and infections.4,5
Once stabilized, the patient was
transported directly from the ED
to the operating room (OR) for a
laparoscopic appendectomy under
general anesthesia. Intraoperative
findings confirmed an inflamed
appendix with a localized peri-
appendiceal abscess, but without
generalized peritonitis. The abscess
was drained surgically, and the peri-
toneal cavity was irrigated thoroughly.
Postoperatively, the patient’s pain was
well controlled with acetaminophen
and minimal opioid use. She toler-
ated clear liquids within a few hours
and advanced to a regular diet
the following day. Her vital signs
remained stable, and the nurses
monitored her closely for signs of on-
going infection. The surgical wound
dressings were clean, dry, and intact.
She was discharged 48 hours after
surgery with instructions for inci-
sion care, completion of her antibi-
otic course, pain management, and
activity restrictions. At her 1-week
follow-up appointment, she report-
ed a smooth recovery with improv-
ing energy levels and no lingering
concerns.
Introduction
Appendicitis is inflammation of the
vermiform appendix. The appendix
is a small, finger-like organ located
at the tip of the cecum portion of the
large intestine in the right lower
quadrant of the abdomen. 6 The
appendix can be located in any area
of the abdomen, especially if there is
abnormal development or a related
condition, such as pregnancy or
prior surgeries.6 The function of the
appendix has long been debated. It
is thought to provide an immuno-
protective role, acting as a lymphoid
organ, particularly in younger
individuals. Another theory suggests
that the appendix serves as a reservoir
for beneficial colonic bacteria, or
normal flora, which can help repopu-
late the gut after illness. Conversely,
some argue that the appendix is

Appendicitis is a global disease
with an incidence of 7% to 12% in
the population of the United States
and Europe.7 It is one of the most
common causes of acute abdominal
surgery, and occurs most commonly
between the ages of 5 and 45, although
it can develop at any age.7 Men are at
a higher risk for appendicitis than
women.7 Appendicitis has a more
rapid course in older adults due
to atherosclerosis, gangrene, and
perforation, which are common.7 In
older adults, age-related atheroscle-
rotic changes can reduce baseline
blood flow to the appendix, leaving
less vascular reserve when inflamma-
tion occurs. As a result, appendicitis
in this population often progresses
more quickly and is more likely to
present with complications such as
gangrene, perforation, or abscess
formation. 2 Perforation is also
considerably more common in older
adults with acute appendicitis, with
reported rates ranging from approxi-
mately 32% to 70%, compared with
20% to 30% in younger patients.2
Children younger than 3 years old
have an 80% perforation rate due to
a delay in diagnosis.7 The incidence
of acute appendicitis among older
adults is relatively low compared
with younger populations; however,
mortality is higher in this popula-
tion due to the increased number of
comorbidities.
Appendicitis most commonly
presents acutely, with symptoms
developing within 24 hours, but it
can also manifest more gradually
as a chronic condition. Chronic ap-
pendicitis is classified as persis-
tent or recurring inflammation
of the appendix, which causes
mild to moderate pain. This can
lead to scar tissue if left untreated.
Acute appendicitis is considered a
medical emergency, whereas chronic
appendicitis is managed with medi-
cation and monitoring. Some causes
of chronic appendicitis include ob-
struction of the appendix with stool
and fecal accumulation, trauma,
tumors, or enlarged lymph nodes. If
there is perforation with an abscess,
the symptoms may have a slower pre-
sentation and less painful onset.6
This article explores the etiology
of appendicitis, including patient
presentation and assessment, treat-
ment, and evaluation. Additionally,
it highlights the nurse’s role in
patient education, ongoing assess-
ment, and management of care.

Patient presentation
Patients with appendicitis typically
present with initial epigastric or
periumbilical pain that migrates
to the RLQ of the abdomen and is
often described as sharp or localized
pain. The early discomfort in the
umbilical region results from visceral
nerve involvement, while the later
RLQ pain is due to irritation of the
parietal peritoneum as inflammation
progresses. 8
Common signs and symptoms
Nausea and vomiting are common
symptoms of appendicitis. These
often follow the onset of pain, as
appendicitis decreases gastrointestinal
motility. The obstruction of the
appendiceal lumen leads to increased
pressure and bloating in the abdo-
men, resulting in the symptoms of
nausea and vomiting.6 Anorexia is a
hallmark symptom that can help
differentiate appendicitis from other
causes of abdominal pain, such as
pelvic inflammatory disease, inflam-
matory bowel disease, and gastroen-
teritis. 8 Low-grade fever is a sign
indicative of the body’s inflammatory
response; it typically develops after
the onset of pain. 8
Physical assessment findings
Localized muscle rigidity and
generalized guarding in adults are
common due to abdominal wall
irritation. Rebound tenderness and
rigidity in the RLQ are signs that
correlate strongly with the severity of
appendicitis, known as McBurney
point tenderness (see McBurney
point). Rovsing sign, which is RLQ
pain upon palpation of the left side,
indicates peritoneal irritation (see
Assessing for appendicitis).8
Appendiceal ruptures
A sudden decrease in pain can signal
an appendix rupture, as the release
of pressure temporarily relieves pain.
A rigid, board-like abdomen is a
key indicator of rupture, suggesting
peritonitis due to the spread of infection
into the peritoneal cavity. 8
Timely diagnosis and intervention
are critical, as a ruptured appendix
can lead to life-threatening peritoni-
tis. Clinical findings combined with
imaging, such as ultrasonography or
computed tomography (CT), confirm
the diagnosis.8

based on a thorough history and

confirm the diagnosis and rule out
other possible causes.6 Key diagnos-
tic criteria to include in the patient’s
health history are signs and symp-
toms such as RLQ pain, fever, nausea,
vomiting, and anorexia. Questions
about health history should also be
addressed.6 Physical assessment
should include abdominal palpation

Assessing for appendicitis
Focused abdominal and musculoskeletal assessment may reveal the following
findings:
• Psoas sign: Right lower quadrant (RLQ) pain elicited with passive extension of
the right hip or with active hip flexion against resistance, suggesting irritation of
the psoas muscle.
• Obturator sign: RLQ pain elicited with passive internal rotation of the flexed
right hip, indicating irritation of the obturator internus muscle.
• McBurney point tenderness: Localized RLQ tenderness at McBurney point,
often accompanied by rebound tenderness and abdominal rigidity. These
findings correlate with increased severity of appendiceal inflammation.
• Rovsing sign: RLQ pain elicited during palpation of the left lower quadrant,
consistent with peritoneal irritation.

Preoperative nursing care
Adequate nursing assessment and
accurate nursing diagnoses upon
admission are essential for developing
and implementing a patient-centered,
high-quality care plan. A thorough
initial assessment enables nurses to
identify the patient’s needs, prioritize
interventions, and anticipate potential
complications. This foundation
ensures that nursing care is tailored
to the individual, promoting optimal
outcomes and enhancing overall
patient satisfaction.11
The nursing assessment of patients
with appendicitis focuses on the
clinical presentation, imaging results,
and, in some cases, laboratory test
results. Physical assessment findings
include tenderness at McBurney point,
rebound tenderness, and guarding.
Based on the initial assessment, the
most appropriate nursing diagnoses
for a patient with appendicitis before
an appendectomy may vary (see Priority
nursing problems in acute appendicitis).

• Fluid balance: The nurse documents normal fluid volume, ensuring that the

Treatment options
The treatment of appendicitis has
traditionally been surgical, first
proposed by Reginald Heber Fitz
in 1886 and refined by McBurney
in 1889 with the development of
the McBurney incision.8 Surgical
approaches have advanced over
time, from open appendectomy to
laparoscopic techniques and, more
recently, to single-incision surgery,
endoscopic retrograde appendicitis
therapy, and natural orifice translu-
minal endoscopic surgery. Open
appendectomy was the standard
treatment for acute appendicitis
until laparoscopic appendectomy
emerged in 1981.8 Initially, laparoscopic
surgery was limited to uncompli-
cated cases due to concerns about
higher rates of intra-abdominal
abscesses in complicated cases.
However, recent evidence shows
no significant difference in abscess
formation between the two methods.8
Laparoscopic appendectomy is now
preferred due to its benefits, includ-
ing shorter hospital stays, fewer com-
plications, and lower rates of surgical
site infections. Current guidelines
recommend laparoscopic appendec-
tomy for all cases of acute appendici-
tis across all age groups.8,12
The review by Krzyzak and
Mulrooney12 emphasizes that early
surgical intervention, such as an
appendectomy, remains the criterion
standard for treatment. Conservative
management with antibiotics can
be effective in some instances of
uncomplicated appendicitis; how-
ever, it is associated with a higher
recurrence rate.14 Newer approaches,
such as laparoscopic appendectomy,
offer advantages, including same-
day discharge, reduced complica-
tions, and faster recovery times.12,15
Appendectomy is performed either
as a laparoscopic approach (inser-
tion of an endoscope to view the
abdominal contents) or a laparotomy
(surgical opening of the abdomen).
A laparoscopic appendectomy requires
a small incision through which the
laparoscope is inserted. The proce-
dure allows for direct visualization of
the appendix and enables definitive
diagnosis with a laparotomy. With
the laparoscopic procedure, hospi-
talization is short, postoperative
complications are infrequent, and
recovery and return to normal
activities are rapid. 6
Immunocompromised patients
are more prone to complications
if treatment is delayed by more than
24 hours. This group includes patients
with diabetes, those who are positive
for human immunodeficiency virus
(HIV) or have a diagnosis of acquired
immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS),
patients who have had transplants,
and patients with a cancer diagnosis.
The laparoscopic appendectomy is
the preferred method of treatment for
this group, given its lower risk of com-
plications and decreased length of
stay. A multidisciplinary approach
is needed when caring for patients,
as several different team members are
required to manage care.16

Effective pain management
supports comfort, mobility, and
recovery. Postoperative pain is man-

medications as prescribed. With the

less tissue trauma and may require
lower doses of opioids. Nurses
should perform comprehensive pain

Patient education is a key com-
ponent of postoperative care and
discharge planning. Nurses should
instruct patients on incision care,
including keeping the site clean and
dry and reporting signs of infection
such as erythema, edema, increased
warmth, purulent drainage, bleeding,
worsening abdominal pain, or fever
greater than 100.4 °F (38 °C). Activity
restrictions, including lifting and driv-
ing limitations, should be reviewed,
along with guidance on gradually
resuming activities of daily living.19
Patients should be instructed to
report a persistent fever of 101 °F
(38.3 °C) or higher, chills, worsening
abdominal pain unrelieved by medi-
cation, significant incision changes
(such as erythema extending more
than 2 cm, foul-smelling drainage,
or wound dehiscence), or persistent
nausea or vomiting. Education
should include proper dressing
change techniques, and patients
should demonstrate understanding
before discharge. Documentation
should reflect pain intensity levels
using a 0 to 10 scale, wound appear-
ance, vital signs, and the patient’s
understanding of discharge in-
structions and when to contact the
provider. Discharge timing varies
based on disease severity (see Risk
factors for prolonged length of stay follow-
ing appendectomy). Patients without
appendiceal rupture are typically
discharged once bowel function
returns and oral intake is tolerated.
Patients with a ruptured appendix
may require a longer hospitalization
for IV antibiotics. If discharged on
oral antibiotics, nurses must empha-
size the importance of completing
the full prescribed course.20
Common nursing diagnoses
following appendectomy include
risk for infection related to the
surgical incision, risk for deep vein
thrombosis related to immobility,
altered bowel function related to
analgesic use and decreased activity,
and risk for nosocomial infection
related to recent hospitalization.6
Through continuous assessment,
evidence-based interventions, and
comprehensive patient education,
nurses play a critical role in promot-
ing recovery and minimizing postop-
erative complications following
appendectomy.
Conclusion
Caring for a patient with appendicitis
requires a timely, collaborative
approach that combines clinical
knowledge, evidence-based practice,
and compassionate nursing care.
From the initial assessment and
diagnosis to surgical intervention,
postoperative recovery, and ongoing
evaluation, nurses play a vital role in
supporting patients through every
step of their plan of care. By staying
alert to potential complications,
managing pain effectively, and
promoting healing through patient
education and supportive care,
nurses can help ensure the best
possible patient outcomes. As health
care continues to evolve, the nurse’s
role in managing appendicitis remains
essential in providing safe, effective,
patient-centered care. n

• Older age
• Elevated preoperative white blood cell (WBC) count
• Elevated preoperative C-reactive protein (CRP)
• Lower preoperative hemoglobin (Hgb)
• Conversion from laporascopic to open surgical procedure
• Longer operative time
