Management of complications after
appendectomy: Literature review
Camila Bras Harriott, MD, Emmanuel E. Sadava, MD∗
Introduction
Acute appendicitis is one of the most prevalent surgical entities at emergency department,
and has an overall lifetime risk of 7%-8%.1 Although it can be discussed whether to operate or
not on these patients, its current mainstay therapy is appendectomy and it is the most com-
monly performed procedure within an acute care surgery service.1 While the mortality rate af-
ter appendectomy has decreased significantly in recent years, the morbidity rate still remains
substantial.
Patients with acute appendicitis are classified as uncomplicated or complicated based on
pre-operative, intra-operative, and/or histopathological findings. Uncomplicated appendicitis is
defined as an inflamed appendix without signs of gangrene, perforation or intra-peritoneal
purulent fluid. As a counterpart, complicated appendicitis applies to all cases with either
a gangrenous inflamed appendix with or without perforation, intraabdominal abscess, peri-
appendicular contained phlegmon, or purulent free fluid. Classification is necessary because
treatment strategies might differ.2 Both open and laparoscopic approach are commonly used
for appendectomy, and the implementation of one over the other depends on multiple variables
such as patient’s condition, type of appendicitis and surgical team expertise among others. Sev-
eral studies3-6 have shown the benefits of laparoscopic appendectomy as compared to the open
approach, such as shorter hospital stay, less postoperative pain, earlier return to daily activities
and lower wound infection rate. Furthermore, patients showed a strong preference for minimally
invasive surgery and high satisfaction after surgery. Bondi et al.4 suggests that with the required
expertise and equipment laparoscopy should be considered the initial choice for most cases of
suspected appendicitis. Independently of the approach, it is important to note that appendec-
tomy is an urgent procedure that could develop several postoperative complications, therefore,
the primary approach may determine how to manage each unfortunate event.

Complications could be defined as any deviation from the postoperative during the first 30
postoperative days. Long-term complications as incisional hernias might also occur. Complica-
tions after appendectomy can occur in up to 17% of patients, including surgical site infection
(SSI), intra-abdominal abscess (IAA), incisional hernias, ileus, bowel obstruction, bleeding, or
stump appendicitis.7 The presence of any of them are often related to longer postoperative re-
covery and increased healthcare expenses.
In this review, we describe early and late complications that may arise after an appendec-
tomy. Current strategies for their management are also discussed. We aimed to provide a com-
prehensive overview that could serve as a valuable resource for the care of patients undergoing
appendectomy.
Type of complication and resolution
Intraabdominal abscess (IAA)
Intraabdominal abscess is the most feared complication after appendectomy, and its inci-
dence is highly variable ranging between 4.2%-28%.8 , 9 IAA occurs more frequently among pa-
tients with complicated appendicitis and increases morbidity rates with longer hospital stay
and needs of complementary treatment. In the presence of postoperative fever, abdominal pain
and/or leukocytosis, an IAA should be suspected. In these patients an abdominal imaging such
as ultrasound or CT scan should be performed to confirm the diagnosis. CT scan is the preferred
modality since it provides the needed information for therapeutic guidance. Several studies have
analyzed risk factors.8 , 10 , 11 for the development of IAA. Obesity, CRP levels greater than 200
mg/L, leukocyte count greater than 17,0 0 0mm3, pelvic peritonitis, and perforated appendicitis
are considered independent risk factors for postoperative IAA.
Upon the diagnosis of IAA, antibiotic therapy, percutaneous drainage, reoperation or a com-
bination of them might be needed.12-14 Therapy can be decided based on the size, localization,
and number of collections. A step-up management of IAA is mandatory to avoid unnecessary
procedures.13 Conservative treatment with antibiotic therapy seems a reasonable option for one
IAA smaller than 3 cm. On the other hand, isolated antibiotic therapy is much less effective
when abscesses are larger than 3 cm in size. In those cases, percutaneous drainage by interven-
tional radiology has become the treatment of choice. In general, percutaneous abscess drainage
is performed in patients with fluid collections of more than 3 cm in diameter and appropri-
ate clinical indications, with use of local anesthesia and sedation.15 Limitations of percutaneous
drainage include cost, availability in hospitals and abscesses in locations that are difficult to ac-
cess.16 Also, it is no free from risks such as bowel perforation, fistula formation, hemorrhage,
and postprocedural sepsis.17
Cultures play an essential role in surgical and post-procedural management, particularly in
cases involving IAA. Although there is no consensus regarding intraoperative cultures sampling,
this step could be important for identifying the microorganisms responsible for abscess forma-
tion. This information guides the selection of appropriate antimicrobial therapy, ensuring tar-
geted treatment and minimizing the risk of antibiotic resistance.18
Some patients with postoperative IAA might require surgical resolution such as those with
multiple collections and/or percutaneous drainage failure.14 , 19 Although Casas et al. found that
complications after appendectomy requiring a surgical therapy are infrequent, laparoscopy is an
effective tool for diagnosis and treatment.19 Even though laparoscopic approach has several ben-
efits in postoperative outcomes, some surgeons advocate open laparotomy with thorough irriga-
tion when percutaneous drainage is ineffective or contraindicated. However, open approach has
shown numerous drawbacks, including incisional pain, elevated rates of wound infections, risk of
incisional hernias, and suboptimal cosmetic outcomes.20 Laparoscopy offers the ability to explore
the entire abdominal cavity and when necessary, drain multiple sites of abscesses.14 Addition-
ally, some studies have demonstrated that laparoscopy results in reduced pain, shorter length

Overall, a step-up approach seems to be the most reasonable strategy for the management of
IAA. Beginning with no invasive strategies as the first approach, failure in treatment can go up
to 16% for antibiotic therapy only, 12.5% for percutaneous drainage and 12.5% for surgery.13 Non-
operative alternatives could be effective in most of the cases, leading to reoperation, preferable
laparoscopic, in cases where percutaneous drainage is not feasible or has failed.
Surgical site infection
Surgical site infection (SSI) is one of the most common complications after appendectomy
and its incidence reaches up to 11%.22 It can be a superficial infection involving the skin only or
more serious infections, usually after the fifth postoperative day.23 Laparoscopy has gained popu-
larity in terms of preventing this complication, but it is still elevated. A multicenter prospective
study has shown a reduction in wound infection rate when comparing open and laparoscopic
approaches (8.7% vs 4.2%, respectively).24 Nevertheless, a recent meta-analysis in pediatric pa-
tients did not find statistically significant differences in postoperative wound infections between
laparoscopic and open appendectomy (odds ratio [OR], 0.63; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.34-
1.15; P = 0.13).25
Considering that appendectomy is not a clean procedure, the management of SSI begins with
meticulous postoperative wound care, facilitating the prompt identification of any potential is-
sues. Sterile dressing changes and constant monitoring for signs of infection, such as redness,
leakage, and purulent discharge are vital. If cultures were obtained during the operation, is
mandatory to adjust the antibiotic therapy as SII is probably associated with the same mi-
croorganisms.18 For superficial wounds, drainage and antibiotics are the initial step in managing
wound infections.26 Otherwise, if no improvement is observed, a surgical debridement of the
wound should be performed and the infectious department should also be consulted.
Appendix retrieval is probably a key step in preventing SSI since the passage of the speci-
men through the skin or ports could be a contamination source. In concordance, a study has
demonstrated that the use of wound irrigation using wet gauze after fascia closure has a ben-
eficial effect on reducing post-operative SSI.27 On the other hand, Turner et al.,28 performed a
comparative analysis of the usefulness of retrieving bag in laparoscopic appendectomy. The au-
thors included a total of 10.357 patients, and they found that the use of retrieval bag during
laparoscopic appendectomy was not associated with a decrease in SSI rates.
SSI can generally be controlled with local wound management and antibiotics in most of
cases. The relevance of this complication is not only due to its relatively high incidence, but also
due to the negative impact in patient’s recovery and return to daily activities. Therefore, despite
being considered a minor complication, all alternatives in preventing SSI based on surgical team
experience and common sense should be encouraged.
Ileus and bowel obstruction
Postoperative ileus or bowel obstruction after appendectomy is not a frequent complication.
Nevertheless, they are reported as the third most common cause of unplanned readmission, with
rates reaching up to 10%.29 Employing minimally invasive techniques and avoiding unnecessary
dissection maneuvers are essential to prevent these complications. In concordance, a compara-
tive study analyzed the readmissions rate for small bowel obstruction after appendectomy com-
paring open and laparoscopic approaches. The authors found that the laparoscopic approach has
significant advantages over the open technique (0.4% vs 1.0%, P = 0.0015).30
Imaging studies, preferably CT scan, can help distinguishing ileus from bowel obstruction.
Additionally, CT can also rule out potential causes of ileus in which a specific treatment is re-
quired, such as intra-abdominal free fluid or IAA. Early mobilization, reduction in opioid use, and

the implementation of enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) protocols have shown promising
results in preventing and managing ileus.31 Additionally, prokinetic agents can also accelerate
the return of normal bowel function. Once the ileus is established, several treatments including
bowel rest, fluid and ionic reposition, nasogastric decompression, chewing gum, drinking coffee
or even re-laparoscopy have been described to address this complication. Ngowe et al.,32 found
that chewing gum after open appendectomy stimulates bowel function, promoting faster oral
intake and reducing length of hospital stay.
In cases of recurrent or persistent obstruction, surgical intervention might be necessary. La-
paroscopy is emerging as the preferred approach in centers with extensive experience in mini-
mally invasive techniques.33 The use of laparoscopy for small bowel obstruction is controversial
due to concerns about reduced working space and the potential for iatrogenic injuries resulting
from bowel distention. However, laparoscopy should be considered in surgeons’ armamentar-
ium following the case-by-case basis.34 In hemodynamically unstable patients with an acute ab-
domen, laparoscopy may not be the initial choice; on the contrary, it is strongly recommended
for stable patients with non-resolving symptoms. Laparoscopic exploration and adhesiolysis, a
key component for addressing adhesive small bowel obstruction, have been shown to be a safe
alternative when conservative and non-operative measures fail. To optimize the success of la-
paroscopic adhesiolysis, several predictive factors have been recognized: history of ≤2 previous
laparotomies, non-median incisions in previous laparotomies, prior appendectomy as the pri-
mary surgery leading to adhesions, the presence of a single band adhesion, prompt laparoscopic
management within 24 hours of symptom onset, absence of peritonitis signs on physical exam-
ination, and surgeon’s experience in performing laparoscopic procedures.35 , 36
Overall, both ileus and bowel obstruction respond to several causes and their management
requires an appropriate recognition and experience of the surgical team. Imaging, mainly CT
scan, plays an important role in ruling out causes that need surgical intervention and it should
not be delayed. In stable patients, laparoscopy allows diagnostic and treatment ensuring optimal
outcomes for patients with adhesive small bowel obstruction.
Bleeding
Bleeding after appendectomy is rare but is a potentially serious complication that requires
prompt and appropriate management. Potential areas of bleeding include the abdominal cavity,
retroperitoneum, digestive tract, or port sites.37 Gastrointestinal hemorrhage, occurring either
early or years post-appendectomy, is typically managed through ligation of the bleeding ves-
sel or cecal resection via emergency laparotomy or laparoscopy. In selected cases, angiographic
embolization stands as an alternative consideration for effective management. The significance
of early recognition and appropriate intervention cannot be overstated in mitigating the risks
associated with post-appendectomy bleeding.
Few studies have described the incidence and treatment of post appendectomy bleeding.
Among the literature, the reported incidence is 2.8%, although it is likely underreported.38 The
literature contains limited cases detailing patients experiencing postoperative bleeding after ap-
pendectomy.37 , 39 , 40 In cases where the bleeding is intraluminal, resolution can be achieved en-
doscopically. Instances of bleeding from an ulcer in the appendiceal stump have been docu-
mented in medical literature. This approach allows for direct visualization and targeted interven-
tion, such as the application of hemoclips and the use of a biological tissue adhesive, providing
a less invasive means to address the specific source of bleeding in the appendiceal stump.40 On
the other hand, when hemoperitoneum or retroperitoneal bleeding are evident, the appropriate
course of action involves surgical intervention either through laparotomy or laparoscopy to ad-
dress the source of bleeding and control. Thus, surgical exploration is crucial for managing cases
where non-operative measures may not suffice to address the severity of the bleeding and its
potential impact on the patient’s overall condition.41
Finally, post-appendectomy bleeding is an unusual condition that is in most cases self-limited
showing scarce GI hemorrhage or hematoma localized in the surgical area. However, it could

Incisional hernias
As in any other abdominal surgery, the risk of developing an incisional hernia (IH) after ap-
pendectomy is latent. It has been observed that up to 15% of patients could develop an IH after
laparotomy and several risk factors have been described such as emergency surgery, hemor-
rhage, trauma, obesity, abdominal sepsis and type, and length of the incision among others.42 , 43
It is assumed that oblique incisions habitually used for open treatment of acute appendicitis
has reduced risk for incisional hernia. In concordance, several studies have shown a rate lower
than 2% for IH when a McBurney incision was implemented.44 , 45 In addition, Drs Beltran and
Cruces42 performed a retrospective analysis in 4593 patients who undergo open appendectomy
with McBurney incision and found that SSI, diabetes and female gender were independent risk
factors for developing an incisional hernia.
The laparoscopic approach has shown to reduce IH rates in abdominal surgery since small in-
cisions are used.46 , 47 However, against any thoughts, trocar site hernias (TSH) seem to be more
frequent than expected ranging from 0.5%-2% depending on the type of surgery analyzed.48 One
potential explanation could be that the umbilical area is the most frequent localization of tro-
car placement and the umbilicus is a well-known risk factor for ventral IH.48 , 49 Furthermore,
a misdiagnosed umbilical hernia and also the presence of rectus diastasis at the umbilical area
could alter the wound healing process leading to IH development.48 , 50 A recent study conducted
by Hernandez et al.51 showed an incidence of umbilical TSH after laparoscopic appendectomy of
20.3%, all of which were diagnosed with CT scan. This finding is alarming and highlights the
necessity of IH prevention. For instance, the occurrence of TSH is reduced when employing non-
bladed trocars. In addition, a key preventive measure involves the meticulous closure of all tro-
car sites larger than 5 mm. This meticulous closure serves as a proactive strategy to mitigate the
risk of trocar site hernia formation.45 , 52 , 53
Surgical repair is often considered the definitive treatment for incisional hernias. While
McBurney incisional hernia is classically approached through open repair, it has been demon-
strated that a minimally invasive surgery (MIS) utilizing a three-port technique and the place-
ment of a composite mesh is equally effective for resolving the condition.54 Similar outcomes
have been observed for the treatment of ventral incisional hernia repairs in the midline via
minimally invasive approach.55 Nowadays, MIS in hernia repair has gained popularity and most
hernia surgeons prefer minimally invasive techniques to manage mid-sized ventral hernias.55 , 56
There is no doubt that IH and TSH after appendectomy are relevant complications, and major
efforts are needed to prevent them. Once the hernia is established, a ventral hernia repair with
mesh reinforcement with MIS is the suggested treatment approach.
Stump appendicitis
Stump appendicitis is a long-term complication after appendectomy that consists of the in-
flammation of the remaining appendix. The lifelong probability of developing stump appendicitis
is low (1/50,0 0 0), representing a rare condition.57 Signs and symptoms are the same as acute ap-
pendicitis and its diagnosis is usually delayed. Along the literature, the time between the incom-
plete appendectomy and stump appendicitis vary between 2 months and 20 years.58 Risk factors
for developing stump appendicitis are the presence of a complicated appendicitis at the initial
appendectomy, improper identification of the appendicular base, and leaving a stump larger than
0.5 cm.19 , 59 After the suspicion arises, a CT scan is the method of choice for the correct diagno-
sis.
Surgery is often needed for treating this complication. Laparoscopic or open completion of
the appendectomy is the adequate treatment.60 Generally, a more extensive procedure should

not be required if the appendiceal stump is easily identified, and the cecum does not exhibit
significant inflammation. Most reported cases in the literature involve open completion appen-
dectomy, with ongoing debates about the inversion of the remaining stump versus simple lig-
ation. Other authors suggest that, unless there are mitigating reasons, laparoscopic completion
appendectomy guided by CT findings is a feasible approach.61 Manatakis et al.61 observed that
delayed diagnosis of stump appendicitis contributed to the need for more extensive resections,
including ileocecectomy or right hemicolectomy in up to 11% of cases. The need for major resec-
tions is related to gangrene, perforation, or significantly inflammatory process of the cecum.
The use of minimally invasive techniques remains technically feasible, even following an ini-
tial open procedure in the hands of experienced surgeons. Laparoscopy allows for effective in-
tervention while minimizing the invasiveness of the surgical procedure.
Conclusion
Appendectomy is a commonly performed surgical procedure that carries inherent risks of
complications. A careful balance between preventive measures, early recognition, and appro-
priate interventions are critical to mitigate their impact. An adequate postoperative monitoring
with rationale used of imaging studies, facilitates the timely identification of complications and
guides for the optimal management.
