ABSTRACT
Cervical cancer is a major global health issue, ranking as
the fourth most common cancer in women worldwide.
Depending on stage, histology, and patient factors, the
standard management of cervical cancer is a combination
of treatment approaches, including (fertility- or non-
fertility- sparing) surgery, radiotherapy, platinum-based
chemotherapy, and novel systemic therapies such as
bevacizumab, immune checkpoint inhibitors, and antibody-
drug conjugates. While ambitious global initiatives seek
to eliminate cervical cancer as a public health problem,
the management of cervical cancer continues to evolve
with major advances in imaging modalities, surgical
approaches, identification of histopathological risk
factors, radiotherapy techniques, and biomarker-driven
personalized therapies. In particular, the introduction of
immune checkpoint inhibitors has dramatically altered the
treatment of cervical cancer, leading to significant survival
benefits in both locally advanced and metastatic/recurrent
settings. As the landscape of cervical cancer therapies
continues to evolve, the aim of the present review is to
provide a comprehensive discussion of the current state
and the latest practice-changing updates in cervical
cancer.
INTRODUCTION
Cervical cancer is the fourth most common cancer
in women worldwide and the most common gyne-
cological cancer globally. 1 Despite the effectiveness
of the human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination and
screening,2 3 cervical cancer remains a major global
health issue, with a particularly high burden in low-
and middle-income countries due to wide disparities
in HPV vaccination coverage, access to preventive
interventions, and socioeconomic factors.4 5 The
5-year overall survival rate for all stages combined is
67%: 91% for early stage, 60% for locally advanced
disease, and 19% for metastatic disease.6 Almost
all cases of cervical cancer (~95%) are caused by
persistent infection with high-risk HPV. The most
common histologies are squamous cell carcinoma
(~80%) and adenocarcinoma (~20%), while other
histologic subtypes remain rare.7 8
Cervical cancer is generally curable if diagnosed
and treated at an early stage. The recommended
treatment for early-stage cervical cancer (Interna-
tional Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO)
2018 stages IA–IB2, and IIA1) is surgery followed by
observation or adjuvant therapy (radiotherapy±con-
current chemotherapy) based on histopathologic
risk factors on definitive pathology. Conservative
and fertility- sparing approaches may be considered
in carefully selected patients. Standard of care for
locally advanced cervical cancer (FIGO 2018 stages
IB3, IIA2–IVA) is definitive concurrent chemoradio-
therapy.9–11 However, recent findings have the poten-
tial to improve the standard of care of patients with
locally advanced cervical cancer. Based on results of
the KEYNOTE-A18 trial, the US Food and Drug Admin-
istration (FDA) recently approved pembrolizumab in
addition to concurrent chemoradiotherapy for patients
with high- risk locally advanced cervical cancer.12
Moreover, results from the INTERLACE trial presented
at the 2023 European Society for Medical Oncology
(ESMO) 	congress 	demonstrated 	that 	induction
chemotherapy before standard concurrent chemo-
radiotherapy provides a significant improvement
in overall survival. 13 Patients with metastatic (FIGO
2018 stage IVB), persistent, or recurrent cervical
cancer should be treated at tertiary referral centers
in a multidisciplinary setting. The management of
these patients poses a major clinical challenge and
relies on combined treatment modalities, including
platinum- based chemotherapy±bevacizumab±pem-
brolizumab, locoregional treatments (surgery, radio-
therapy), enrollment in clinical trials, and/or palliative
care. 9–11
The WHO Global Initiative was launched to accel-
erate the elimination of cervical cancer as a public
health problem by using three main pillars: HPV
vaccination, effective screening (Pap test, HPV test,
colposcopy), and treatment of precancerous lesions.
Meanwhile, there have been continuous and signif-
icant improvements in imaging modalities, surgical
approaches (tailored parametrectomy, less radical
surgery, sentinel lymph node (SLN) biopsy), patho-
logical diagnosis, radiotherapy techniques (image-
guided, intensity-modulated radiation), and novel
treatment options with targeted agents, including
immune checkpoint inhibitors and antibody-drug
conjugates.
Given recent practice-changing advances in the
management of cervical cancer, this narrative review
aimed to summarize the contemporary state and
latest updates in the management of cervical cancer.

METHODS
A formal systematic review was not performed, but relevant liter-
ature in cervical cancer was identified by searching PubMed for
English- language articles from inception to March 2024. Priority
was given to the selection of randomized controlled trials, system-
atic reviews, meta- analyses, and practice guidelines. The refer-
ences of identified articles were also reviewed. Selected articles
were mutually agreed by the authors. Details of the study design
and results of the pivotal studies underpinning treatment recom-

Epidemiology
Cervical cancer is the fourth leading cause of cancer incidence
and mortality in women worldwide, with more than 85% of cases
occurring in low-resource settings.14 In 2022, there were an esti-
mated 661 021 cervical cancer cases and 348 189 deaths glob-
ally.1 The cumulative lifetime risk of developing and dying from
cervical cancer are 1.50% and 0.79%, respectively.1 The burden of
cervical cancer incidence is not equal worldwide, with a clear soci-
oeconomic gradient. In countries with a low human development
index, cervical cancer incidence and mortality rates are three and
six times higher, respectively, than in countries with a high human
development index.15 Specific regions of the world with particu-
larly high incidence and mortality rates include East and Southeast
Asia (57.3% of global cases, 57.6% of deaths), sub-Saharan Africa
(16.6% of cases, 19.3% of deaths), and Latin America (9.2% of
cases, 8.5% of deaths).15
The incidence of cervical cancer by stage varies internationally
as well, with patients in low human development index countries
more likely to be diagnosed at a later stage. Worldwide, around
40–50% of cervical cancers are diagnosed at an early stage,
35–40% at locally advanced stage, and 10–25% at metastatic
stage.16 Geographic and socioeconomic disparities in the incidence
and global burden of cervical cancer reflect inequalities in the avail-
ability of the HPV vaccine and accessibility to adequate screening
and treatment of precancerous lesions. The main risk factor for
cervical cancer is persistent HPV infection, with HPV 16 and 18
being the most common high-risk genotypes. 17 More than 70% of
cervical cancer diagnoses are attributed to HPV 16/18 genotypes,
while other known risk factors include smoking, higher number
of sexual partners, and HIV infection, highlighting the importance
of risk reduction strategies aimed at smoking cessation and HIV
prevention.18
Pathophysiology
High- risk HPV is a sexually transmitted virus capable of estab-
lishing persistent infections leading to precancerous lesions and
cervical cancer. HPV is a non-enveloped, double-stranded DNA
virus of 8 kilobase pairs containing eight open reading frames,
including two structural genes (L1, L2) and six early genes involved
in viral replication (E1, E2, E4, E5, E6, E7).19 20 After HPV infection,
distinct mRNA splicing patterns and multiple promoters control the
expression of viral genes, allowing expression of gene products at
different stages of the viral life cycle.
E6 and E7 have an essential function during the viral life cycle,
increasing viral fitness and virion production. 19 The E6 protein is
able to directly interact with p53, a tumor suppressor protein, and
induce ubiquitin- dependent proteolysis. The E7 protein binds to pRb
suppressor protein to facilitate the release of transcription factors
such as E2F and induce gene transcription, resulting in disruption
of cell cycle regulatory pathways, resistance to apoptosis, and
dysregulated cell proliferation. 21 22 Persistent HPV infection leads to
integration of the viral genome into the cellular genome, promoting
sustained expression of E6 and E7, and loss of the E2 gene, a
repressor of the expression of these oncoproteins (Figure 1). Addi-
tionally, HPV integration induces genetic alterations, amplification
of oncogenes, chromosomal rearrangements, and instability.22
Most cervical cancer cases demonstrate integrated HPV genomes.
In cases where the genome is in an episomal form, dysregula-
tion occurs by epigenetic modifications such as DNA methylation,
histone modifications, non-coding RNA regulation and chromatin
regulation. 23
Prevention and Screening
In 2020, WHO launched a global strategy to eliminate cervical
cancer as a public health problem by 2130, with the goal of a global
incidence of less than 4 per 100 000 women per year. The strategy
focuses on three key pillars: (1) prevention through HPV vaccina-
tion; (2) screening and treatment of precancerous lesions; and (3)
adequate treatment of invasive cervical cancer. WHO countries are
expected to achieve the following three main targets by 2030 to
reduce the median cervical cancer incidence by 10%: (1) 90% of
girls fully vaccinated against HPV by 15 years of age; (2) 70% of
women screened with high-performance tests for HPV detection
by 35 years of age and again by 45 years of age; and (3) 90% of
women adequately treated for precancerous lesions.24
Prophylactic HPV vaccination has been shown to decrease
the burden of HPV-associated disease. Currently, there are five
commercial prophylactic HPV vaccines: two bivalent vaccines (HPV
16 and 18), two tetravalent vaccines (HPV 6, 11, 16, and 18), and
one nonavalent vaccine (HPV 6, 11, 16, 18, 31, 33, 45, 52, and 58).
In addition, there is emerging evidence on the safety and immu-
nogenicity of a new nonavalent vaccine produced in Escherichia
coli, a simple and cost-effective system for the production of L1
virus- like particles, with potential to alleviate supply constraints
and affordability of HPV vaccine.20 25 26 WHO recommends either
a single or two-dose schedule in women under 20 years of age to
improve vaccination coverage. 27 28 The primary target of vaccina-
tion is girls aged 9–14 years, prior to the start of sexual activity.
Vaccination of secondary targets, such as males and older females,
is also recommended where feasible and affordable.
Primary screening strategies are a critical component of cervical
cancer prevention and include cervical cytology (Pap smear),
HPV co- testing, and colposcopy (with biopsy). The main objec-
tive of screening programs is to detect precancerous lesions to
provide prompt treatment and appropriate follow-up before cancer
develops. 20 About 50% of cervical cancers occur in women with
inadequate screening.29 In a recent estimate of screening coverage
across 202 countries, 1.6 billion (67%) of 2.3 billion women
aged 20–70 years had never been screened for cervical cancer.
According to Bruni et al, 69% of countries surveyed have official
recommendations for primary cervical cancer screening, with only
29% sending individual reminders to women.30
The use of polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-based tests for HPV
genome detection has proven to be cost-effective while increasing
sensitivity for detecting high-grade lesions.20 31 32 Several countries

Clinical Presentation
The most frequent clinical presentation of cervical cancer is vaginal
discharge/bleeding, postcoital bleeding, or intermittent spotting.
Early- stage cervical cancer is often asymptomatic or associated
with vague and nonspecific symptoms that are typically identified
during screening programs. 38 39 Cervical cancer spreads locally into
the vagina, parametrium, uterine corpus, and nearby organs, as well
as through lymphatic channels to regional and distant lymph nodes.
Metastasis can also occur via hematogenous routes to the lungs,
liver, and skeleton.40 Advanced stages may present with a wide
range of signs and symptoms, including hematuria, malodorous
vaginal discharge, dyspareunia, and pelvic or back pain. Pelvic
sidewall involvement may lead to a triad of lower limb edema, flank
pain, and sciatica. Rarely, vesicovaginal or rectovaginal fistulas are
encountered during initial presentations.41

Treatment
Treatment planning should be made in specialized referral centers
within a multidisciplinary setting and be tailored to each patient.
Patients should be carefully selected and counseled on the standard
treatment and potential alternatives, aligning expectations. Treat-
ment depends on several factors: (a) disease-related factors (stage
at diagnosis, histology, tumor size, parametrial invasion, lymph
node involvement); (b) patient-related factors (age, performance
status, desire for fertility, medical comorbidities); (c) resources
available at the treating facility (multidisciplinary team, experience,
financial resources). Treatment options may vary across countries
depending on national guidelines, resources, and reimbursement
policies.10 56 Squamous cell carcinomas and usual-type (HPV-
associated) adenocarcinomas are typically treated in a similar
manner. 57–59
Early-stage Cervical Cancer
The standard primary treatment of early- stage (FIGO 2018
stages IA–IB2, IIA1) cervical cancer is surgery followed by
observation or adjuvant therapy based on histopatholog-
ical risk factors on final pathology (Figure 2). Conservative
surgical approaches should be considered after careful patient
selection. Fertility- sparing approaches should be reserved for
patients with a desire for childbearing and low- risk disease
(Figure 3). Conservative and fertility- sparing approaches
should be considered only within a multidisciplinary setting
with expert gynecological oncologists, pathologists, and radi-
ologists to identify appropriate candidates. Definitive radio-
therapy 	(±concurrent 	platinum- containing 	chemotherapy)
may be a valid alternative to surgery in patients deemed to
have inoperable cancer due to poor performance status. 9–11
Surgery
Three major concepts in the treatment of early- stage cervical

Surgical Approach
In 2018, a phase III, multicenter, randomized trial (Laparoscopic
Approach to Cervical Cancer—LACC) reported higher recurrence
rate and worse overall survival with minimally invasive surgery for
early-stage cervical cancer, establishing the open approach as the
standard of care (Table 1).60 The rate of disease-free survival at
4.5 years was 86.0% with minimally invasive surgery and 96.5%
with open surgery, a difference of −10.6 percentage points (95%
CI −16.4 to −4.7). The final overall survival analysis has recently
been published and showed that the overall survival rate at 4.5
years was 90.6% versus 96.2% for the minimally invasive and open
surgery groups, respectively (HR for death of any cause 2.71; 95%
CI 1.32 to 5.59; p=0.007).61
Potential theories for these findings have been proposed,
including: (1) the impact of CO2 pneumoperitoneum; (2) the lack
of use of peritoneal protective maneuvers to avoid tumor expo-
sure; and (3) the use of a uterine manipulator. 62 63 To further clarify
this topic, two ongoing randomized controlled trials, RACC 64 and
ROCC (NCT04831580), are investigating the oncological safety of
a robotic versus an open approach. These studies do not allow the
use of an intrauterine manipulator and require documentation of
standardized surgical techniques for tumor containment.
Conservative Approaches
The type of hysterectomy and extent of parametrectomy (type A-C2
according to the 2017 revised Querleu-Morrow classification) have
traditionally been based on preoperative prognostic risk factors
(tumor size, maximum stromal invasion, and lymphovascular space
invasion), which are used to categorize tumors at low, intermediate,
and high risk of recurrence. 65 Recent advances in the surgical
management of cervical cancer have identified selected patients
who may benefit from less radical surgery for early-stage cervical
cancer.

The ConCerv trial, a prospective, single-arm, multicenter study,
examined the feasibility of conservative surgery to reduce surgical
complications and morbidity in low-risk, early- stage cervical
cancer (Table 1).66 Eligibility criteria included: (1) FIGO 2009 stage
IA2–IB1 cervical cancer; (2) squamous cell (any grade) or adeno-
carcinoma (grade 1 or 2 only) histology; (3) tumor size <2 cm; (4) no
lymphovascular space invasion; (5) depth of invasion <10 mm; (6)
negative imaging for metastatic disease; and (7) negative coniza-
tion margins. Cervical conization was performed to determine
eligibility, with one repeat cone permitted. Patients who met these
inclusion criteria underwent a second surgery with pelvic lymph
node assessment (if fertility preservation was desired) or a simple
hysterectomy with lymph node assessment. The 2- year cumulative
recurrence rate in the study population was 3.5% (95% CI 0.9% to
9.0%). 66
The randomized phase III SHAPE trial recently evaluated simple
hysterectomy compared with radical hysterectomy in a non-
inferiority design (Table 1).67 This study included patients with
squamous cell carcinoma, adenocarcinoma, or adenosquamous
carcinoma of any histologic grade, with or without lymphovas-
cular space involvement, FIGO 2009 stage IA2 or IB1 with tumors
measuring no more than 2 cm, and with limited depth of cervical
stromal invasion (invasion of <50% of stromal tissue or to a depth of
<10 mm, or both). Notably, 80.2% of patients had undergone diag-
nostic loop electrosurgical excision procedure or conization before
surgery. Findings demonstrated simple hysterectomy was not infe-
rior to radical hysterectomy with respect to the 3-year incidence of
pelvic recurrence (absolute difference of 0.35 percentage points;
90% CI −1.62 to 2.32).67
Results from the GOG 278 trial were presented at the 2024
Society of Gynecologic Oncology (SGO) annual meeting (Table 1).68
This phase I/II multicenter prospective trial aimed to evaluate the
impact of non-radical surgical treatment, either simple hysterec-
tomy or cone biopsy (for fertility preservation) and pelvic lymph-
adenectomy, on functional outcomes of bladder, bowel, and sexual
function and on the incidence and severity of lymphedema in
patients with FIGO 2009 stage IA1 (lymphovascular space invasion
present) and IA2–IB1 (≤ 2 cm) cervical cancer with depth of stromal
invasion ≤10 mm and no evidence of metastasis on radiologic
staging. Of the 201 patients eligible for survival analysis, only three
experienced a recurrence, suggesting that non-radical surgery for
early- stage cancer appears safe and is associated with low periop-
erative morbidity.

Fertility-sparing Approaches
Cervical cancer is frequently diagnosed in women of reproduc-
tive age, with one third of patients younger than 45 years, and
40% nulliparous or with only one child at diagnosis.81 Thus, there
is a growing need for fertility-sparing strategies for women with
cervical cancer who have not yet completed childbearing. Fertility-
sparing approaches may be considered in highly selected patients
who have been thoroughly counseled regarding oncologic and
obstetric risks (Figure 3). Consultation at a center with experience
in oncofertility and discussion of each case in a multidisciplinary
team are essential before offering fertility-sparing treatment. The
specific goal of fertility-sparing surgery is complete resection of the
tumor with adequate free margins and preservation of the upper
cervix.
Fertility-sparing surgical options include cervical conization,
simple trachelectomy, vaginal radical trachelectomy, and radical
abdominal trachelectomy. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy followed
by conservative surgery is considered an alternative approach,
although without any prospective evidence to confirm its onco-
logic safety. Negative lymph node status is required for fertility-
sparing approaches and preoperative imaging has a crucial role
to rule out nodal involvement. Surgical lymph node staging follows
the same principles of non-fertility-sparing management and can
be performed through a minimally invasive approach. Surgical
lymph node staging can be performed using different approaches
based on national guidelines and availability of resources: (1) SLN
biopsy (with or without frozen section) and fertility-sparing proce-
dure in the same surgery; (2) systematic pelvic lymphadenectomy
and fertility-sparing procedure in the same surgery; (3) two-step
approach first performing lymph node assessment (either SLN or
systematic pelvic lymphadenectomy) and then fertility-sparing
surgery only in patients with negative lymph nodes on definitive
pathology.9–11 Intraoperative placement of permanent cerclage
should be performed after trachelectomy. Routine hysterectomy
after completion of childbearing is not mandatory.
Vaginal radical trachelectomy has traditionally been the most
widely used fertility- sparing procedure since its development
in the early 1990s,82 with recurrence, pregnancy, and live birth
rates for tumors <2 cm of 4.7%, 58.7%, and 71%, respectively. 83
Patients with favorable tumor features (tumor size ≤2 cm, absence
of lymphovascular space invasion, depth of invasion <10 mm, and
negative lymph nodes) have a risk of parametrial involvement
<1% 84 85 and can be excellent candidates for more conservative
procedures (conization or simple trachelectomy). 86 87 The ConCerv66
and GOG 278 trials68 reported on fertility outcomes of patients with
cervical cancer treated with conization and pelvic node assess-
ment and suggested that this conservative strategy is safe and can
lead to successful fertility outcomes in selected patients with low-
risk early-stage cervical cancer (Table 1). In the ConCerv trial, 14
pregnancies have been reported among 11 of 40 women (27.5%)
who underwent fertility-sparing surgery and remained in the study.
Of these 14 pregnancies, 13 (92.9%) delivered at term.66 Among
the 68 patients who underwent fertility-sparing surgery and were
eligible for analysis in the GOG 278 trial, 31 patients attempted to
conceive and 16 pregnancies (15 patients) were obtained, including
nine full- term pregnancies.68
Fertility-sparing approaches are contraindicated in tumors >2 cm
and should be considered experimental for selected FIGO 2018
stages IB2–IB3.88 Low recurrence rates have been observed after
radical trachelectomy using an abdominal laparotomic approach
(4.8%); however, laparotomy has been associated with poor fertility
outcomes (pregnancy rate: 36%; live birth rate: 66.6%). 83 Platinum-
based neoadjuvant chemotherapy before fertility-sparing surgery
has been explored to shrink tumors that would not be amenable to
upfront fertility-sparing approaches.89 90 This multimodal approach
is associated with better obstetric outcomes, with a pregnancy
rate of 74.5% and a live birth rate of 78.7% 83

Adjuvant Therapy
Adjuvant management after surgery for early-stage cervical cancer
is still largely debated and should be individualized based on histo-
pathological risk factors on definitive pathology (Figure 2). Patients
with at least one of three high-risk GOG-109 criteria (Peters criteria),
such as positive margins, lymph nodes, or parametria, have a
high risk of recurrence (25–30%) and adjuvant platinum-based
chemoradiotherapy with or without vaginal brachytherapy boost is
accepted as standard of care (Table 1).94 Notably, recent results of
the NRG oncology/RTOG 0724/GOG-0724 study presented at the
2024 American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) Annual Meeting
showed no survival benefit for the addition of systemic chemo-
therapy following chemoradiotherapy in patients with cervical
cancer and high-risk factors after radical hysterectomy (Table 1).95
In the absence of high-risk criteria, patients meeting at least two
of intermediate-risk GOG- 92 criteria (Sedlis criteria), such as lymph
vascular space invasion, >1/3 stromal invasion, and bulky tumor
(largest dimension ≥4 cm), have historically been considered at
intermediate risk of recurrence (15–20%) and receive adjuvant
external pelvic radiotherapy±concurrent chemotherapy±vaginal
brachytherapy boost (Table 1). 96–98 Patients who do not meet either
criterion have generally been considered at low risk of recurrence.
However, the Sedlis criteria were established 25 years ago, and
since then there have been tremendous advances in imaging modal-
ities, surgical approaches, radiotherapy techniques, and diagnostic
and molecular pathology. The population analyzed by Sedlis et al
in 1999 is likely to be different from patients with cervical cancer
undergoing hysterectomy today. There is currently wide heteroge-
neity in the definition of intermediate-risk and low-risk early-stage
cervical cancer across centers. Several alternative criteria, such
as modified Sedlis criteria or the GOG score, have been developed
over time to guide postoperative decision making in clinical prac-
tice. 99 100 The best management for patients meeting only one of
the Sedlis criteria and/or having other potentially important risk
factors (high grade, age<50 years, non-squamous histology, Silva
pattern of stromal invasion, pathological tumor-free distance, close
surgical margins) remains controversial.
Postoperative management in the intermediate-risk population
is still largely debated and heterogeneous. The benefit of adding
chemotherapy to radiotherapy seems to be minimal.101 Moreover,
the need for adjuvant therapy has recently been questioned. A
recent sub-analysis of the SCCAN study evaluating 692 patients
(274 observed after surgery and 418 who received radiotherapy or
chemoradiotherapy) suggested that patients with intermediate-risk
early-stage cervical cancer who are treated with adequate radical
surgery alone achieve similar survival rates compared with those
who receive adjuvant therapy.102 The 5- year disease-free survival
(83.2% vs 80.3%; p=0.365) and overall survival (88.7% vs 89.0%;
p=0.281) were not significantly different between the two groups. A
recent meta-analysis by Gómez- Hidalgo et al also reported similar
recurrence and mortality rates for patients undergoing observation
versus adjuvant treatment.103
Reaching an evidence-based consensus for the definition and
management of intermediate-risk early-stage cervical cancer has
important practical implications. Avoiding unnecessary combined
regimens with surgery and radiotherapy whenever possible
should be part of the therapeutic goals to preserve quality of
life and sexual function. Several ongoing trials (CERVANTES/
ENGOT- cx16 (NCT04989647),104 ACCEPT (NCT05277688), GOG
263 (NCT01101451, NCT04723875)) are currently investigating the
role of different types of adjuvant therapy after surgery for early-
stage cervical cancer.
Locally Advanced Cervical Cancer
Standard Treatment
Concurrent cisplatin-containing chemoradiation plus brachytherapy
is the standard of care for patients with locally advanced cervical
cancer or patients who are not candidates for surgery. In 1999,
five clinical trials demonstrated that concurrent chemoradiation
reduced the risk of death by 30–50% compared with radiotherapy
alone.94 105–108 A subsequent Cochrane meta-analysis reported
a 19% relative reduction in the risk of death with chemoradio-
therapy compared with radiotherapy (HR 0.81; 95% CI 0.71 to 0.91;
p<0.001), which translates into an absolute survival benefit of 6%
(from 60% to 66%) at 5 years.109 Figure 4 shows the primary treat-
ment algorithm for locally advanced cervical cancer (FIGO 2018
stages IB3, IIA2–IVA).
Fertility preservation options such as oocyte, embryo or ovarian
tissue preservation, and oocyte donation should be discussed
with patients. Ovarian transposition may be considered before
pelvic radiotherapy to preserve endocrine function for selected
patients <45 years of age with squamous cell cancer or usual-type
(HPV- associated) adenocarcinoma, except in the case of positive
nodes. 110–113
Para-aortic Lymph Node Staging
Historically, it has been widely debated whether para-aortic
lymphadenectomy provides a therapeutic benefit or has a diag-
nostic role to avoid extended- field radiotherapy in patients with
clinically negative para-aortic lymph nodes. 80 Routine adoption of
this approach into clinical practice is highly heterogeneous among
institutions worldwide.
However, the role of lymphadenectomy has been strongly chal-
lenged by recent evidence. The prospective multicenter randomized
Uterus-11 trial compared surgical (pelvic and para-aortic lymph-
adenectomy) versus imaging (abdominal CT and/or MRI and chest
radiograph, CT or PET-CT) staging prior to primary chemoradiation
for locally advanced cervical cancer and demonstrated no statis-
tically significant difference in disease-free survival between the
two groups after a median follow-up of 90 months (Table 1). 114
Therefore, systematic para-aortic lymphadenectomy should not be
recommended. Extended-field para-aortic radiation can be consid-
ered based on the presence and number of positive pelvic nodes
(more than two level I nodes or at least one level II (common iliac)
nodes) or tumor size (>2 cm). 115–117

tool has the potential to further improve the reliability of preoperative

Radical Surgery versus Chemoradiotherapy
Radical surgery (with or without neoadjuvant chemotherapy) as a
potential alternative to standard concurrent chemoradiotherapy for
locally advanced cervical cancer remains controversial.121–123 Two
recent randomized clinical trials (Gupta et al 124 and EORTC 55994 124
)
failed to demonstrate superiority of the neoadjuvant chemotherapy-
surgery approach compared with concomitant chemoradiotherapy;
therefore, concomitant chemoradiotherapy remains the standard of
care for locally advanced cervical cancer (Table 1).
Nonetheless, radical surgery after neoadjuvant chemotherapy
may yield non-inferior overall survival outcomes with acceptable
toxicity and health-related quality of life in selected patients with
FIGO 2018 stage IB3/IIA2 cervical cancer. 124 125 In particular, radical
surgery may be considered an alternative approach to radio-
therapy for patients with FIGO 2018 stage IB3/IIA2 disease in the
following scenarios: (1) young patients who decline radiotherapy
for concerns about possible radiotherapy-induced toxicity and
want to preserve overall quality of life and sexual function; (2) poor
availability and/or accessibility to adequate radiotherapy facilities,
especially in low-income countries; (3) contraindications to radio-
therapy (active inflammatory bowel disease, inability to maintain
patient positioning, prior radiotherapy).10 126 127 Patients should be
discussed by a multidisciplinary team and counseled about the
advantages and disadvantages of both treatment options (surgery
vs radiotherapy). To avoid the combination of radical surgery and
postoperative radiotherapy, decision making regarding adjuvant
management after radical surgery should not be rigorously based
on traditional selection criteria for adjuvant treatment, but should
be individualized.11

Metastatic, Persistent, or Recurrent Cervical Cancer
The management of metastatic, persistent or recurrent cervical
cancer poses a significant clinical challenge and depends on
multidisciplinary assessment of clinical factors and patient goals,
including age, performance status, location and extent of disease,
prior treatment modalities, treatment-free interval, and patient
preferences. The standard of care is based primarily on systemic
therapies and enrollment in clinical trials is strongly encouraged.
For recurrent cervical cancer, repeat biopsy could be considered
to perform molecular/genetic analysis and identify targetable
biomarkers, including PD-L1, MMR, HER2, tumor mutational
burden, NTRK and RET gene fusion testing. Early involvement of a
palliative care specialist is advised. Best supportive care should be
provided in patients unfit to receive further treatments.9 11

Disease Amenable to Local Treatment
The treatment algorithm for recurrent cervical cancer amenable to
local therapy, including locoregional and oligometastatic disease,
is outlined in Figure 6. Multifocal local relapse or multiple/distant
metastases (non-oligometastatic disease) not amenable to local
therapies should be treated like metastatic disease. Locore-
gional pelvic recurrences are classified into central (if the tumor
is confined to the vagina, bladder, rectum, and/or parametrium) or
non- central/lateral (if the tumor involves the muscles and vascu-
lature of the lateral pelvic wall). Oligometastatic disease refers to
cases with one to five metastatic or recurrent lesions.9 148 149 In
the case of suspected tumor persistence or recurrence, histological
verification by biopsy is strongly recommended if feasible before
treatment planning.
The treatment for locoregional relapse depends on whether the
patient has had previous radiotherapy. Previously irradiated patients
should be considered for surgery. Pelvic exenteration is generally
indicated as the last therapeutic option for patients with advanced,
persistent, or recurrent cervical cancer and should be considered
an individualized approach after careful patient selection and coun-
seling by a multidisciplinary team in specialized centers.9–11 150 It
is generally recommended for central pelvic recurrence (<2 cm)
in the absence of pelvic sidewall involvement, extrapelvic nodes,
or peritoneal disease. Extended pelvic surgery (laterally extended
endopelvic resection)±intraoperative radiotherapy can also be
considered in carefully selected cases with pelvic sidewall involve-
ment. In the era of targeted therapies, systemic therapy could be
considered before and/or after surgery for locoregional recurrence.
For patients who wish to avoid pelvic exenteration, systemic treat-
ment alone remains a viable option, especially in the bevacizumab/
immunotherapy-naïve setting. Individualized re-irradiation with
external beam radiotherapy±chemotherapy can be also considered
an alternative to surgical resection for locoregional relapse after
primary radiotherapy.
Radiotherapy-naïve patients and those with outside- field relapses
should be treated with external beam radiotherapy±chemotherapy-
±brachytherapy. Surgical resection±intraoperative chemotherapy is
another option. Brachytherapy alone might be considered for small
superficial vaginal relapses (<5 mm thickness). For highly selected
patients with isolated distant metastases (nodal, lung, liver, bone),
either at initial diagnosis or at relapse, amenable to local therapy,
combined locoregional treatment modalities including surgery,
external beam radiotherapy±concurrent chemotherapy, and/or
local ablative therapies might be considered, possibly followed by
adjuvant systemic therapy.9–11

Prognosis and Surveillance
For all comers with cervical cancer, 5-year overall survival is around
67%. The prognosis of early-stage disease is significantly better
than later stages, with 5-year overall survival rates of >80–90%
for early stages, ~60% for locally advanced cervical cancer,
30–35% in cases of positive locoregional lymph nodes, and<20%
for distant metastatic disease. Approximately one-third of women
with cervical cancer experience a recurrence during follow-up, with
most relapses (>75%) occurring within the first 2–3 years after
treatment.9–11
Oncologic follow-up aims to detect recurrence and/or secondary
cancers, monitor quality of life, manage long-term sequelae, and
provide holistic supportive care. There is no standard protocol for
post- treatment follow-up, which should be individualized based
on individual prognostic factors, treatment modalities (fertility-
sparing approaches), symptoms, and personal preferences.151 Only
26–36% of recurrences are diagnosed during routine follow-up, 152
while the majority of recurrences are symptomatic. 153 A reasonable
approach for patients with cervical cancer who have had complete
response after primary treatment includes: (a) clinical re-evalua-
tion every 3–6 months for the first 2 years, every 6–12 months
for another 3–5 years, then annually; (b) imaging assessment (US,
CT, MRI, PET-CT) every 6–12 months for the first 2 years, then
annually at least for other 3 years. 152 154 Up to 45–50% of relapses
are localized within the pelvis, while locoregional extrapelvic and
distant metastases are less frequent. Therefore, physical exam-
ination is crucial for cervical cancer surveillance and can lead to
the diagnosis of asymptomatic recurrence in 29–75% of cases.155
Cervical/vaginal cytology tests are not recommended because of
low recurrence detection rates (0–17%) 153 and the risk of false-
positive results after radiotherapy. 156 157
Recently, the annual recurrence risk model (ARRM) has been
proposed for tailored surveillance in patients with early-stage
cervical cancer based on the patient’s risk status and respective
annual recurrence risk. 158 Hormonal therapy with estrogen can be
considered in survivors with postmenopausal symptoms.159 160
CONCLUSIONS
While international efforts aim to eliminate cervical cancer as a
global health problem by promoting widespread access to preven-
tion and screening, recent years have seen major advances in
the treatment of cervical cancer, including sophisticated imaging
modalities, less radical surgical approaches, improved molecular
diagnostics, modern radiotherapy techniques, and biomarker-
driven personalized therapies. These achievements have led to a
new era in the management of cervical cancer. Tailoring surgical
and radiation approaches to early-stage cervical cancer allows for
optimization of oncologic outcomes while reducing morbidity and
toxicity in patients who are often young and for whom preserving
quality of life and desire for childbearing is essential. The advent of
immunotherapy has dramatically improved the prognosis of patients
with advanced and recurrent cervical cancer, and additional novel
agents, such as antibody-drug conjugates, are expected to further
enrich treatment options.
